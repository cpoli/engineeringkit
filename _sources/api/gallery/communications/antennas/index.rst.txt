    <div class="sphx-glr-thumbcontainer" tooltip="Friis first wrote his transmission formula not with gains but with the antennas&#x27; effective areas: P_r/P_t = A_t A_r/(d\lambda)^2. A receiving antenna collects the power falling on its effective area A_e, and reciprocity ties that area to its gain, A_e = G\lambda^2/4\pi (~engineeringkit.communications.effective_aperture). For a dish of physical area A and aperture efficiency \eta_a, G = 4\pi\eta_a A/\lambda^2 (~engineeringkit.communications.aperture_gain). A dish of fixed size gains 6 dB at every doubling of frequency, while an antenna of fixed gain collects less and less power.">

    <div class="sphx-glr-thumbcontainer" tooltip="The half-wave dipole is the reference antenna of radio engineering. With the sinusoidal current a thin wire carries, its far field varies as \cos(\tfrac\pi2\cos\theta)/\sin\theta (~engineeringkit.communications.half_wave_dipole_pattern), a little narrower than the \sin\theta of a short dipole. Integrating the radiated power gives a directivity of 1.64, or 2.15 dBi (~engineeringkit.communications.half_wave_dipole_directivity), and a radiation resistance of 73 ohms (~engineeringkit.communications.half_wave_dipole_resistance). Philip Carter of RCA put such calculations in circuit form in 1932: his induced-EMF method gave the self- and mutual impedances of dipoles, the basis of every array design that followed.">
