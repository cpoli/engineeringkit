    <div class="sphx-glr-thumbcontainer" tooltip="Claude Shannon&#x27;s &quot;A mathematical theory of communication&quot; (1948) measured information by entropy, H = -\sum p\log_2 p. For a binary source entropy_binary peaks at one bit when both symbols are equally likely. The same paper defined channel capacity. A binary symmetric channel that flips each bit with probability p has capacity 1 - H(p) (~engineeringkit.communications.binary_symmetric_capacity): even a very noisy channel can carry information reliably at a reduced rate, and a channel with p = 1/2 carries nothing.">

    <div class="sphx-glr-thumbcontainer" tooltip="In &quot;Communication in the presence of noise&quot; (1949) Shannon proved that a channel of bandwidth B with white Gaussian noise and signal-to-noise ratio S/N can carry C = B\log_2(1 + S/N) bits per second with arbitrarily small error (~engineeringkit.communications.shannon_capacity), and no more. Writing S/N = (E_b/N_0)(C/B) gives the minimum energy per bit for a given spectral efficiency. As bandwidth becomes unlimited the minimum tends to \ln 2, or -1.59 dB, the Shannon limit.">

.. raw:: html

    <div class="sphx-glr-thumbcontainer" tooltip="The same 1928 paper that set the limit of 2B pulses per second also said which pulse shapes reach it without intersymbol interference: those whose spectrum, copied at every multiple of the symbol rate, adds up to a constant. The ideal sinc pulse meets the criterion with the least bandwidth but decays as 1/t, so any timing error lets many neighbours leak in. The raised-cosine family (~engineeringkit.communications.raised_cosine_pulse, raised_cosine_spectrum) spends an excess bandwidth \alpha/2T on a cosine roll-off that keeps the criterion and makes the tails fall as 1/t^3. Split into two root-raised-cosine filters (~engineeringkit.communications.root_raised_cosine_pulse), one at each end of the link, it also gives the receiver a matched filter.">

    <div class="sphx-glr-thumbcontainer" tooltip="In Robert Fano&#x27;s information theory class at MIT, David Huffman was offered a term paper instead of a final exam: find the most efficient binary code. Shannon and Fano&#x27;s top-down splitting was good but not always optimal. Huffman worked bottom-up. He merged the two least likely symbols into one, and repeated until a single node remained. The resulting prefix code (~engineeringkit.communications.huffman_code) has the least average length of any symbol-by-symbol code, within one bit of the entropy (~engineeringkit.communications.entropy), and reaches it exactly when every probability is a power of one half.">
