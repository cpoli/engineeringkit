    <div class="sphx-glr-thumbcontainer" tooltip="On Christmas Eve 1906 Reginald Fessenden is said to have broadcast voice and music from Brant Rock, Massachusetts, by varying the amplitude of a continuous high-frequency wave. Conventional AM, s(t) = A_c[1+\mu m(t)]\cos 2\pi f_c t (~engineeringkit.communications.am_signal), keeps the carrier so a simple envelope detector can recover the message, provided the modulation index stays at or below one. The spectrum shows the cost: a strong carrier and two sidebands, so at most one third of the power carries the message.">

    <div class="sphx-glr-thumbcontainer" tooltip="In 1915 John Renshaw Carson of AT&amp;T showed that the carrier in AM carries no information. Multiplying the message by the carrier alone, s(t) = m(t)\cos 2\pi f_c t (~engineeringkit.communications.dsb_sc), puts all the transmitted power into the sidebands. The receiver must supply a coherent carrier: mix, low-pass filter, and the message returns, scaled by \tfrac12\cos\phi for a phase error \phi. Carson also showed that one sideband is enough, the basis of single-sideband telephony.">

    <div class="sphx-glr-thumbcontainer" tooltip="Edwin Howard Armstrong (1933-1936) demonstrated that frequency modulation with a deviation much larger than the message bandwidth trades bandwidth for a dramatic reduction of noise and static. The phase of an FM wave is the integral of the message (~engineeringkit.communications.fm_signal); for a tone of modulation index \beta = \Delta f/f_m the spectrum is a comb of Bessel-function sidebands J_n(\beta). Carson&#x27;s rule, B \approx 2(\Delta f + f_m) (~engineeringkit.communications.carson_bandwidth), captures about 98% of the power.">
