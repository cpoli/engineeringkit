    <div class="sphx-glr-thumbcontainer" tooltip="Long-distance telephone repeaters with feedback sometimes &quot;sang&quot;. Harry Nyquist (1932) showed how to predict this from the measured open-loop frequency response alone. Trace L(j\omega) for all frequencies (~engineeringkit.control_systems.nyquist): the closed loop has Z = N + P unstable poles, where N counts clockwise encirclements of -1 (~engineeringkit.control_systems.nyquist_encirclements) and P the unstable open-loop poles.">

    <div class="sphx-glr-thumbcontainer" tooltip="Hendrik Bode (1940, 1945) turned Nyquist&#x27;s criterion into design numbers read off a logarithmic plot of gain and phase (~engineeringkit.control_systems.bode). The gain margin is how much the loop gain can grow before the phase-crossover point reaches -1; the phase margin is how much extra lag the loop tolerates at the gain crossover (~engineeringkit.control_systems.stability_margins). For L = 2/(s(s+1)(s+2)) the phase crosses -180^\circ at \omega = \sqrt2, where |L| = 1/3, so the gain margin is 3. A loop can also cross -180^\circ more than once. Then the margin that matters is the crossover nearest instability, which need not be the first.">

.. raw:: html

    <div class="sphx-glr-thumbcontainer" tooltip="Hendrik Bode defined the sensitivity of a feedback loop, S = 1/(1 + L), as the fraction of a plant change that survives in the closed loop (~engineeringkit.control_systems.sensitivity), and its complement T = L/(1 + L) (~engineeringkit.control_systems.complementary_sensitivity) as the map from reference and sensor noise to the output. Since S + T = 1, the two cannot both be small at one frequency. Bode also showed that, for a stable loop that rolls off at least as 1/s^2, \int_0^\infty \ln|S(j\omega)|\,d\omega = 0. Sensitivity pushed down at low frequency pops up elsewhere, the waterbed effect. Freudenberg and Looze (1985) showed that each unstable open-loop pole p raises the integral to \pi\sum \mathrm{Re}\,p.">

    <div class="sphx-glr-thumbcontainer" tooltip="Nathaniel Nichols, at the MIT Radiation Laboratory during the war, plotted the open-loop gain in decibels against its phase. On those axes the closed-loop gain of a unity-feedback loop, |L/(1+L)|, is a fixed family of contours (~engineeringkit.control_systems.nichols_closed_loop), so one curve shows both. The highest contour the locus touches is the closed-loop resonant peak M_r (~engineeringkit.control_systems.nichols), and reshaping the locus away from the critical point (-180^\circ, 0\text{ dB}) lowers it. The chart was published in James, Nichols and Phillips&#x27;s Theory of Servomechanisms (1947). Here a lead compensator (~engineeringkit.control_systems.design_lead) lifts the phase of L = 4/(s(s+1)(s+2)) near crossover and pulls the resonant peak down.">
