    <div class="sphx-glr-thumbcontainer" tooltip="In 1826 Georg Simon Ohm drove current from a thermocouple, a steady source, through brass wires of different lengths x and read the current on a torsion galvanometer. The readings fitted X = a/(b + x): a fixed &quot;exciting force&quot; a divided by the total resistance of the wire plus the rest of the circuit, b. In modern form that is V = IR (~engineeringkit.electrical_engineering.ohms_law). This example rebuilds Ohm&#x27;s experiment, recovers the source voltage and internal resistance from the readings, and checks the series and parallel rules that follow from the law.">

    <div class="sphx-glr-thumbcontainer" tooltip="Samuel Hunter Christie described the circuit in 1833, and Charles Wheatstone made it the standard way to measure resistance in his 1843 Bakerian lecture. Two voltage dividers share a source; a galvanometer joins their midpoints. When R_1/R_2 = R_3/R_4 the midpoints sit at the same potential and the galvanometer reads zero, whatever the source voltage (~engineeringkit.electrical_engineering.wheatstone_balanced). A null reading needs no calibrated meter, which is why the bridge is so accurate. This example balances a bridge against an unknown resistor and shows how sharply the null depends on it.">

Writing the current law at every node, with Ohm&#x27;s law for each branch, gives a linear system G\mathbf{v} = \mathbf{i}, nodal analysis. Circuit simulators add one row per voltage source, modified nodal analysis (~engineeringkit.electrical_engineering.nodal_analysis). This example solves a two-source ladder network, checks both of Kirchhoff&#x27;s laws on the result, and verifies superposition.">
