Examples
========

.. include:: /_generated/nav/electrical_engineering.rst

This gallery walks through every public feature of
``engineeringkit.electrical_engineering``, one breakthrough per script: DC
circuits and nodal analysis, equivalent networks, transients, AC circuits,
electronics, machines, transmission lines and power systems.

:doc:`Units, assumptions and numerical validation </guides/electrical_engineering>`
gives the physical validity limits. Each script is self-contained and can be
run directly with ``python examples/electrical_engineering/<section>/<script>.py``.

Sections
--------

- **dc_circuits** -- Ohm's law, Wheatstone's bridge and Kirchhoff's nodal analysis.
- **networks** -- Jacobi's maximum power theorem, Thévenin and Norton equivalents.
- **transients** -- Kelvin's telegraph cable, Helmholtz's RL current rise and Kelvin's oscillatory RLC discharge.
- **ac_circuits** -- Stanley's transformer, Steinmetz's phasors and Lodge's resonance.
- **electronics** -- Ragazzini's operational amplifier and Shockley's diode with its load line.
- **machines** -- The Hopkinsons' DC machine and Steinmetz's induction motor equivalent circuit.
- **transmission_lines** -- Heaviside's telegrapher's equations and the Smith chart.
- **power** -- Tesla's three-phase system, Fortescue's symmetrical components and fault currents, Budeanu's reactive power, swing curves and the equal-area criterion, and the Gauss-Seidel and Newton-Raphson load flows.

    <div class="sphx-glr-thumbcontainer" tooltip="Around 1840 Moritz von Jacobi, building battery-powered electric motors in St Petersburg (one drove a boat on the Neva), asked what load draws the most power from a battery. A source of EMF V and internal resistance R_s delivers P = V^2 R_L/(R_s + R_L)^2 to a load R_L, largest when R_L = R_s (~engineeringkit.electrical_engineering.maximum_power_transfer). At that match the source wastes as much as it delivers, so the efficiency is only 50%. Engineers later learned to read the theorem the other way: power grids run far from the match, at high efficiency, while radio receivers match to capture every last picowatt.">

    <div class="sphx-glr-thumbcontainer" tooltip="Before the first transatlantic cable was laid, William Thomson (later Lord Kelvin) analysed how a signal spreads along a submarine cable. In 1855 he showed that, with negligible inductance, the line obeys a diffusion equation: a pulse arrives smeared over a time proportional to rc\,\ell^2, the resistance and capacitance per unit length times the length squared, the law of squares. The building block is the single RC section, whose voltage rises as 1 - e^{-t/RC} (~engineeringkit.electrical_engineering.rc_charging_voltage). This example checks the time constant of one section, then chains sections into a cable and confirms the law of squares.">

    <div class="sphx-glr-thumbcontainer" tooltip="Kelvin&#x27;s cable theory ignored inductance. Oliver Heaviside, a self-taught former telegraph operator, kept all four line constants: series resistance R and inductance L, shunt conductance G and capacitance C per metre. In 1876 he wrote the telegrapher&#x27;s equations, whose sinusoidal waves decay and travel according to \gamma = \sqrt{(R + j\omega L)(G + j\omega C)} (~engineeringkit.electrical_engineering.propagation_constant). In 1887 he found that if R/L = G/C (~engineeringkit.electrical_engineering.distortionless_condition), every frequency is attenuated equally and travels at the same speed, so a signal arrives weaker but undistorted. Real cables have far too little inductance; Pupin&#x27;s and Campbell&#x27;s loading coils (1899-1900) added it and made long-distance telephony possible. This example compares a cable with and without enough inductance.">
