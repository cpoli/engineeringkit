Examples
========

.. include:: /_generated/nav/electrical_engineering.rst

This gallery walks through every public feature of
``engineeringkit.electrical_engineering``, one breakthrough per script: DC
circuits and nodal analysis, equivalent networks, transients, AC circuits,
transmission lines and power systems.

:doc:`Units, assumptions and numerical validation </guides/electrical_engineering>`
gives the physical validity limits. Each script is self-contained and can be
run directly with ``python examples/electrical_engineering/<section>/<script>.py``.

Sections
--------

- **dc_circuits** -- Ohm's law, Wheatstone's bridge and Kirchhoff's nodal analysis.
- **networks** -- Jacobi's maximum power theorem, Thévenin and Norton equivalents.
- **transients** -- Kelvin's telegraph cable and the RC time constant.
- **ac_circuits** -- Stanley's transformer, Steinmetz's phasors and Lodge's resonance.
- **transmission_lines** -- Heaviside's telegrapher's equations and the Smith chart.
- **power** -- Tesla's three-phase system, Fortescue's symmetrical components, Budeanu's reactive power and the digital load flow.

    <div class="sphx-glr-thumbcontainer" tooltip="In March 1886 William Stanley, working for George Westinghouse, lit the shops of Great Barrington, Massachusetts, from a 500 V alternator through transformers that stepped the voltage down to 100 V at each building. His design, refined from the Gaulard-Gibbs secondary generator and the Hungarian ZBD transformer of 1885, had a closed iron core and windings in parallel across the line. An ideal transformer scales voltage by the turns ratio, V_2 = V_1 N_2/N_1 (~engineeringkit.electrical_engineering.transformer_secondary), current by its inverse and impedance by its square. This example shows why that made AC distribution win: stepping up the voltage cuts line losses by the square of the ratio.">

At the International Electrical Congress in Chicago, Charles Proteus Steinmetz showed that a sinusoid A\cos(\omega t + \phi) can be carried as the complex number Ae^{j\phi}. Differentiation becomes multiplication by j\omega, so an inductor becomes the impedance j\omega L (~engineeringkit.electrical_engineering.inductor_impedance), a capacitor 1/j\omega C (~engineeringkit.electrical_engineering.capacitor_impedance), and AC circuits are solved by Ohm&#x27;s law with complex numbers (~engineeringkit.electrical_engineering.series_rlc_impedance). This example solves a series RLC circuit by phasors and checks the answer against a time-domain simulation.">

    <div class="sphx-glr-thumbcontainer" tooltip="In 1826 Georg Simon Ohm drove current from a thermocouple, a steady source, through brass wires of different lengths x and read the current on a torsion galvanometer. The readings fitted X = a/(b + x): a fixed &quot;exciting force&quot; a divided by the total resistance of the wire plus the rest of the circuit, b. In modern form that is V = IR (~engineeringkit.electrical_engineering.ohms_law). This example rebuilds Ohm&#x27;s experiment, recovers the source voltage and internal resistance from the readings, and checks the series and parallel rules that follow from the law.">

Two voltage dividers share a source; a galvanometer joins their midpoints. When R_1/R_2 = R_3/R_4 the midpoints sit at the same potential and the galvanometer reads zero, whatever the source voltage (~engineeringkit.electrical_engineering.wheatstone_balanced). A null reading needs no calibrated meter, which is why the bridge is so accurate. This example balances a bridge against an unknown resistor and shows how sharply the null depends on it.">

.. raw:: html

    <div class="sphx-glr-thumbcontainer" tooltip="Around 1840 Moritz von Jacobi, building battery-powered electric motors in St Petersburg (one drove a boat on the Neva), asked what load draws the most power from a battery. A source of EMF V and internal resistance R_s delivers P = V^2 R_L/(R_s + R_L)^2 to a load R_L, largest when R_L = R_s (~engineeringkit.electrical_engineering.maximum_power_transfer). At that match the source wastes as much as it delivers, so the efficiency is only 50%. Engineers later learned to read the theorem the other way: power grids run far from the match, at high efficiency, while radio receivers match to capture every last picowatt.">

The EMF is the open-circuit voltage; the resistance is that voltage divided by the short-circuit current, or the resistance seen with every source zeroed. This example reduces a two-source network with nodal_analysis, builds the equivalent with thevenin_from_source, and checks that the two agree for every load.">

This example checks that the Thévenin and Norton forms are indistinguishable at the terminals, and that converting sources back and forth simplifies a ladder to a single source.">

    <div class="sphx-glr-thumbcontainer" tooltip="In May 1888 Nikola Tesla presented &quot;A new system of alternate current motors and transformers&quot; to the American Institute of Electrical Engineers. Currents displaced in phase produce a rotating magnetic field, which drives an induction motor with no commutator. Polyphase supply has a second advantage that Mikhail Dolivo-Dobrovolsky exploited with three phases in 1889-1891: a balanced three-phase load draws constant instantaneous power, \sqrt3 V_L I_L \cos\phi (~engineeringkit.electrical_engineering.three_phase_power), and the three return currents cancel, so three wires do the work of six. This example checks both, and draws the rotating field.">

    <div class="sphx-glr-thumbcontainer" tooltip="An unbalanced three-phase system, such as one with a fault to ground, couples all three phases and resisted hand calculation. Charles LeGeyt Fortescue of Westinghouse showed in 1918 that any three phasors are the sum of three balanced sets: a zero-sequence set (three equal phasors), a positive-sequence set (a-b-c rotation) and a negative-sequence set (a-c-b) (~engineeringkit.electrical_engineering.symmetrical_components). In a symmetrical network the three sequences do not interact, so each is solved as a single-phase circuit and the results recombined (~engineeringkit.electrical_engineering.phase_components). This example decomposes an unbalanced set and computes a single-line-to-ground fault the way protection engineers still do.">

.. raw:: html

    <div class="sphx-glr-thumbcontainer" tooltip="When a load&#x27;s current lags its voltage, part of the energy flows back and forth every half cycle and does no work. Constantin Budeanu of Bucharest gave this the name reactive power in 1927 and the unit &quot;var&quot; (volt-ampere reactive), adopted by the IEC in 1930. For sinusoids the power triangle holds: real power P = VI\cos\phi (~engineeringkit.electrical_engineering.real_power), reactive power Q = VI\sin\phi (~engineeringkit.electrical_engineering.reactive_power), apparent power S^2 = P^2 + Q^2, and the power factor P/S (~engineeringkit.electrical_engineering.power_factor). Budeanu extended Q to distorted waveforms as a sum over harmonics, which leaves a remainder he called the distortion power D. This example checks the power triangle from waveforms and computes D for a current with harmonics.">

In 1956 J. B. Ward and H. W. Hale of American Gas and Electric published the first practical digital method. They wrote the network as its bus admittance matrix (~engineeringkit.electrical_engineering.admittance_matrix) and improved each bus voltage in turn from the power specified there, a Gauss-Seidel iteration (~engineeringkit.electrical_engineering.gauss_seidel_load_flow). The method is simple and needs little memory but converges slowly, which over-relaxation partly cures; Newton-Raphson methods replaced it in the 1960s. This example solves a five-bus network and checks the solution against a general-purpose nonlinear solver.">

    <div class="sphx-glr-thumbcontainer" tooltip="Before the first transatlantic cable was laid, William Thomson (later Lord Kelvin) analysed how a signal spreads along a submarine cable. In 1855 he showed that, with negligible inductance, the line obeys a diffusion equation: a pulse arrives smeared over a time proportional to rc\,\ell^2, the resistance and capacitance per unit length times the length squared, the law of squares. The building block is the single RC section, whose voltage rises as 1 - e^{-t/RC} (~engineeringkit.electrical_engineering.rc_charging_voltage). This example checks the time constant of one section, then chains sections into a cable and confirms the law of squares.">

Distributed lines, reflections and the Smith chart.

    <div class="sphx-glr-thumbcontainer" tooltip="Kelvin&#x27;s cable theory ignored inductance. Oliver Heaviside, a self-taught former telegraph operator, kept all four line constants: series resistance R and inductance L, shunt conductance G and capacitance C per metre. In 1876 he wrote the telegrapher&#x27;s equations, whose sinusoidal waves decay and travel according to \gamma = \sqrt{(R + j\omega L)(G + j\omega C)} (~engineeringkit.electrical_engineering.propagation_constant). In 1887 he found that if R/L = G/C (~engineeringkit.electrical_engineering.distortionless_condition), every frequency is attenuated equally and travels at the same speed, so a signal arrives weaker but undistorted. Real cables have far too little inductance; Pupin&#x27;s and Campbell&#x27;s loading coils (1899-1900) added it and made long-distance telephony possible. This example compares a cable with and without enough inductance.">

His 1939 chart plots the reflection coefficient \Gamma = (Z_L - Z_0)/(Z_L + Z_0) (~engineeringkit.electrical_engineering.reflection_coefficient) in the unit disc, overlaid with circles of constant normalised resistance and arcs of constant reactance. Moving along a lossless line just rotates \Gamma around the centre, at constant standing-wave ratio (~engineeringkit.electrical_engineering.standing_wave_ratio), so the input impedance (~engineeringkit.electrical_engineering.input_impedance) can be read off the grid. This example uses the chart to design a single-stub match, drawn with plot_smith_chart.">
