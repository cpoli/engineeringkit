    <div class="sphx-glr-thumbcontainer" tooltip="In May 1888 Nikola Tesla presented &quot;A new system of alternate current motors and transformers&quot; to the American Institute of Electrical Engineers. Currents displaced in phase produce a rotating magnetic field, which drives an induction motor with no commutator. Polyphase supply has a second advantage that Mikhail Dolivo-Dobrovolsky exploited with three phases in 1889-1891: a balanced three-phase load draws constant instantaneous power, \sqrt3 V_L I_L \cos\phi (~engineeringkit.electrical_engineering.three_phase_power), and the three return currents cancel, so three wires do the work of six. This example checks both, and draws the rotating field.">

    <div class="sphx-glr-thumbcontainer" tooltip="An unbalanced three-phase system, such as one with a fault to ground, couples all three phases and resisted hand calculation. Charles LeGeyt Fortescue of Westinghouse showed in 1918 that any three phasors are the sum of three balanced sets: a zero-sequence set (three equal phasors), a positive-sequence set (a-b-c rotation) and a negative-sequence set (a-c-b) (~engineeringkit.electrical_engineering.symmetrical_components). In a symmetrical network the three sequences do not interact, so each is solved as a single-phase circuit and the results recombined (~engineeringkit.electrical_engineering.phase_components). This example decomposes an unbalanced set, computes a single-line-to-ground fault the way protection engineers still do and compares the four fault types (~engineeringkit.electrical_engineering.fault_currents).">

    <div class="sphx-glr-thumbcontainer" tooltip="When a load&#x27;s current lags its voltage, part of the energy flows back and forth every half cycle and does no work. Constantin Budeanu of Bucharest gave this the name reactive power in 1927 and the unit &quot;var&quot; (volt-ampere reactive), adopted by the IEC in 1930. For sinusoids the power triangle holds: real power P = VI\cos\phi (~engineeringkit.electrical_engineering.real_power), reactive power Q = VI\sin\phi (~engineeringkit.electrical_engineering.reactive_power), apparent power S^2 = P^2 + Q^2, and the power factor P/S (~engineeringkit.electrical_engineering.power_factor). Budeanu extended Q to distorted waveforms as a sum over harmonics, which leaves a remainder he called the distortion power D. This example checks the power triangle from waveforms and computes D for a current with harmonics.">

    <div class="sphx-glr-thumbcontainer" tooltip="Until the 1950s, utilities computed the flow of power in their networks on AC network analysers, scale models of the grid built from resistors, inductors and capacitors. In 1956 J. B. Ward and H. W. Hale of American Gas and Electric published the first practical digital method. They wrote the network as its bus admittance matrix (~engineeringkit.electrical_engineering.admittance_matrix) and improved each bus voltage in turn from the power specified there, a Gauss-Seidel iteration (~engineeringkit.electrical_engineering.gauss_seidel_load_flow). The method is simple and needs little memory but converges slowly, which over-relaxation partly cures; Newton-Raphson methods replaced it in the 1960s. This example solves a five-bus network and checks the solution against a general-purpose nonlinear solver.">

    <div class="sphx-glr-thumbcontainer" tooltip="As transmission lines grew longer in the 1920s, generators at their two ends began to fall out of step after faults. Westinghouse engineers R. D. Evans and C. F. Wagner computed swing curves, the rotor angle \delta against time, by integrating the swing equation (2H/\omega_s)\,\ddot\delta = P_m - P_{max}\sin\delta step by step (~engineeringkit.electrical_engineering.swing_response). For one machine against an infinite bus there is a shortcut: the kinetic energy gained while the fault holds the electrical power down (the accelerating area) must be given back after clearing (the decelerating area) before \delta passes the unstable equilibrium. Equating the two areas gives the critical clearing angle (~engineeringkit.electrical_engineering.equal_area_clearing_angle) and, for a bolted fault, the critical clearing time (~engineeringkit.electrical_engineering.critical_clearing_time). This example checks the criterion against the integrated swing curves.">

    <div class="sphx-glr-thumbcontainer" tooltip="Gauss-Seidel load flow needs little memory, but on large networks it converges slowly or not at all. Newton&#x27;s method converges quadratically, yet its Jacobian is as large as the network squared. William Tinney and Clifford Hart of the Bonneville Power Administration made it practical in 1967 by keeping the Jacobian sparse and ordering the buses so that its factorization stays sparse too. Newton-Raphson has been the standard power flow method ever since. In polar form the unknowns are the bus angles and the load-bus voltage magnitudes; a generator (PV) bus holds its voltage magnitude and lets its reactive power follow (~engineeringkit.electrical_engineering.newton_raphson_load_flow). This example solves a five-bus network with a generator bus, shows the quadratic convergence against Gauss-Seidel (~engineeringkit.electrical_engineering.gauss_seidel_load_flow) and checks the power balance.">
