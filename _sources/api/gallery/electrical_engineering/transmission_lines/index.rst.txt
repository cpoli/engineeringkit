    <div class="sphx-glr-thumbcontainer" tooltip="Kelvin&#x27;s cable theory ignored inductance. Oliver Heaviside, a self-taught former telegraph operator, kept all four line constants: series resistance R and inductance L, shunt conductance G and capacitance C per metre. In 1876 he wrote the telegrapher&#x27;s equations, whose sinusoidal waves decay and travel according to \gamma = \sqrt{(R + j\omega L)(G + j\omega C)} (~engineeringkit.electrical_engineering.propagation_constant). In 1887 he found that if R/L = G/C (~engineeringkit.electrical_engineering.distortionless_condition), every frequency is attenuated equally and travels at the same speed, so a signal arrives weaker but undistorted. Real cables have far too little inductance; Pupin&#x27;s and Campbell&#x27;s loading coils (1899-1900) added it and made long-distance telephony possible. This example compares a cable with and without enough inductance.">

His 1939 chart plots the reflection coefficient \Gamma = (Z_L - Z_0)/(Z_L + Z_0) (~engineeringkit.electrical_engineering.reflection_coefficient) in the unit disc, overlaid with circles of constant normalised resistance and arcs of constant reactance. Moving along a lossless line just rotates \Gamma around the centre, at constant standing-wave ratio (~engineeringkit.electrical_engineering.standing_wave_ratio), so the input impedance (~engineeringkit.electrical_engineering.input_impedance) can be read off the grid. This example uses the chart to design a single-stub match, drawn with plot_smith_chart.">
