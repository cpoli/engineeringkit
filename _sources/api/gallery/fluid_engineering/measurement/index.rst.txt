Measurement

    <div class="sphx-glr-thumbcontainer" tooltip="In 1732 Henri Pitot lowered a glass tube bent at a right angle into the Seine, its mouth facing upstream, and watched the water inside rise above the river surface. The rise is the velocity head v^2/(2g), and it showed, against the belief of the day, that the current is faster near the surface than at depth. Turned around, the stagnation rise gives the speed, v = \sqrt{2\Delta p/\rho} (~engineeringkit.fluid_engineering.pitot_velocity). This example reads river and air speeds from a Pitot tube and finds the Mach number at which the incompressible formula starts to mislead an airspeed indicator.">

    <div class="sphx-glr-thumbcontainer" tooltip="Giovanni Battista Venturi reported in 1797 that water flowing through a contraction drops in pressure at the narrowest point and recovers most of it in a gentle expansion. Clemens Herschel turned the effect into the Venturi water meter in 1887: continuity and Bernoulli&#x27;s equation give the flow from the pressure drop between inlet and throat, Q = C_d A_2\sqrt{2\Delta p/[\rho(1 - (A_2/A_1)^2)]} (~engineeringkit.fluid_engineering.venturi_flow). This example draws the pressure along a meter and reads the flow back from it.">

The jet narrows just past a sharp-edged hole (the vena contracta), so the flow is a discharge coefficient of about 0.61 times the ideal one (~engineeringkit.fluid_engineering.orifice_flow). This example drains a tank through an orifice (~engineeringkit.fluid_engineering.tank_drain_time) and checks the square-root law of the level against a step-by-step integration.">

    <div class="sphx-glr-thumbcontainer" tooltip="James Thomson, engineer and elder brother of Lord Kelvin, reported to the British Association in 1858 that a V-shaped notch measures small flows better than a rectangular weir. The flow section keeps the same shape as the head changes, so the discharge coefficient hardly varies, and the flow grows as H^{5/2} (~engineeringkit.fluid_engineering.v_notch_weir_flow) instead of H^{3/2} (~engineeringkit.fluid_engineering.rectangular_weir_flow), so a small flow still gives a readable head. This example compares the rating curves of the two weirs and finds how precisely each must be read.">
