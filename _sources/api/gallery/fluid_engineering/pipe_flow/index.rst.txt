The law Q = \pi R^4\Delta p/(8\mu L) (~engineeringkit.fluid_engineering.poiseuille_flow) follows from a parabolic velocity profile (~engineeringkit.fluid_engineering.poiseuille_velocity), and in Darcy&#x27;s form it is the laminar friction factor f = 64/Re (~engineeringkit.fluid_engineering.laminar_friction_factor).">

    <div class="sphx-glr-thumbcontainer" tooltip="Julius Weisbach wrote the friction loss in a pipe in 1845 as h_f = f\,(L/D)\,v^2/(2g), with a coefficient that he fitted to experiments. Henry Darcy&#x27;s tests on new and incrusted cast-iron mains in Paris, published in 1857, showed that the coefficient depends on the diameter and the state of the wall. The Darcy-Weisbach equation (~engineeringkit.fluid_engineering.darcy_head_loss), with loss coefficients for the fittings (~engineeringkit.fluid_engineering.minor_head_loss), is still how pipelines are sized. This example follows the energy and hydraulic grade lines of water flowing from a reservoir through two pipes to a nozzle.">

At low speed the streak stayed a straight thread; above a critical speed it broke up and coloured the whole tube. The critical speed fell as the tube widened and as the water warmed, and Reynolds showed that every case met at one value of \rho v D/\mu (~engineeringkit.fluid_engineering.reynolds_number). This example computes the critical speed against diameter and temperature, and shows the jump in friction factor across the transition.">

The curves share the smooth-pipe law at low Reynolds number (~engineeringkit.fluid_engineering.smooth_pipe_friction_factor), leave it once the grains poke through the viscous sublayer, and settle on a constant that depends only on the roughness, 1/\sqrt f = 1.74 + 2\log_{10}(R/k_s) (~engineeringkit.fluid_engineering.nikuradse_friction_factor). This example draws the two limits and finds where each roughness becomes fully rough.">
