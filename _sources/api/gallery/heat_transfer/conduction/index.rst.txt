    <div class="sphx-glr-thumbcontainer" tooltip="Joseph Fourier&#x27;s 1822 treatise made the heat flux proportional to the temperature gradient, q = -k\,\mathrm{d}T/\mathrm{d}x, derived the heat equation from it, and invented the series that bear his name to solve it. For steady flow through a slab the law gives \dot Q = kA\Delta T/L (~engineeringkit.heat_transfer.fourier_heat_rate), so a slab behaves like an electrical resistor L/(kA) (~engineeringkit.heat_transfer.plane_wall_resistance) and layered walls add like resistors in series (~engineeringkit.heat_transfer.composite_wall). Curved walls give logarithmic resistances, and with them the surprising critical radius of insulation.">

    <div class="sphx-glr-thumbcontainer" tooltip="Air-cooled aero engines of the First World War shed their heat through cylinder fins, and in 1922 D. R. Harper and W. B. Brown of the Bureau of Standards gave NACA the mathematics. A thin fin obeys \theta&#x27;&#x27; = m^2\theta with m = \sqrt{hP/(kA_c)} (~engineeringkit.heat_transfer.fin_parameter); with an insulated tip it carries \sqrt{hPkA_c}\,\theta_b\tanh mL (~engineeringkit.heat_transfer.fin_heat_rate_adiabatic), a fraction \tanh(mL)/mL of what an isothermal fin would (~engineeringkit.heat_transfer.fin_efficiency_adiabatic). Their practical trick: a convecting tip is accounted for by lengthening the fin by half its thickness. This example solves the fin equation numerically and checks both.">

The explicit scheme is stable only for a mesh Fourier number r = \alpha\Delta t/\Delta x^2 \le 1/2; the fully implicit one is always stable but only first order in time. Averaging the two, they got an unconditionally stable, second-order method (~engineeringkit.heat_transfer.conduction_1d). This example measures the order of each scheme and watches the explicit one blow up.">
