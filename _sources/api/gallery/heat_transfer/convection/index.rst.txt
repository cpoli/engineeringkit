Convection
----------

Newton cooling, Nusselt-number correlations for forced and natural convection, laminar tube flow and the heat and mass transfer analogy.

    <div class="sphx-glr-thumbcontainer" tooltip="In 1701 Newton published, anonymously, a temperature scale reaching far beyond the melting point of tin. He heated a block of iron red hot, set it in a steady wind, and timed how long it took to cool. Assuming that the heat lost is proportional to the excess temperature, \dot Q = hA(T - T_\infty) (~engineeringkit.heat_transfer.convection_heat_rate), equal intervals of time should multiply the excess temperature by equal factors (~engineeringkit.heat_transfer.lumped_temperature). Newton then read temperatures off the clock. This example repeats the measurement on a model block, recovers the time constant from the readings, and checks the energy balance.">

    <div class="sphx-glr-thumbcontainer" tooltip="At Berkeley in 1930 Frank Dittus and Llewellyn Boelter correlated tests on automobile radiator tubes with a power law in the Reynolds and Prandtl numbers. In McAdams&#x27;s rounded form, Nu = 0.023\,Re^{0.8}Pr^{n} with n = 0.4 for heating and 0.3 for cooling (~engineeringkit.heat_transfer.dittus_boelter_nusselt), it became the most quoted equation in convection. This example sizes the coefficient for water in a tube, shows that the Colburn analogy between friction and heat transfer reproduces the 0.023, and measures the scatter against Gnielinski&#x27;s modern correlation.">

    <div class="sphx-glr-thumbcontainer" tooltip="Free convection on a vertical plate was long described piecewise: a laminar Ra^{1/4} law, a turbulent Ra^{1/3} law, and a gap between them. In 1975 Stuart Churchill and Humbert Chu fitted one smooth expression to the data from conduction-dominated creeping flow to fully turbulent plumes, Nu = \{0.825 + 0.387 Ra^{1/6}/[1 + (0.492/Pr)^{9/16}]^{8/27}\}^2 (~engineeringkit.heat_transfer.churchill_chu_nusselt, with rayleigh_number). The example checks both asymptotes and the laminar boundary-layer theory it replaces.">

    <div class="sphx-glr-thumbcontainer" tooltip="In 1883 Leo Graetz, measuring the conductivity of liquids flowing through heated tubes, solved for the temperature of a fluid in laminar Poiseuille flow entering a tube whose wall is held at a new temperature. The answer is a series of eigenfunctions; far from the inlet only the first survives and the Nusselt number settles at Nu_D = 3.657 (~engineeringkit.heat_transfer.laminar_tube_nusselt). A uniform wall flux gives 48/11 instead. Near the inlet the thin thermal boundary layer gives higher values, which Hausen fitted as a function of the Graetz number (~engineeringkit.heat_transfer.hausen_nusselt), and the flow is fully developed after the entry length (~engineeringkit.heat_transfer.entry_length). This example marches Graetz&#x27;s problem numerically down the tube and recovers all three.">

    <div class="sphx-glr-thumbcontainer" tooltip="Blasius (1908) had reduced Prandtl&#x27;s laminar boundary layer on a flat plate to one ordinary differential equation, f&#x27;&#x27;&#x27; + \tfrac12 ff&#x27;&#x27; = 0. In 1921 Ernst Pohlhausen, also in Prandtl&#x27;s Göttingen, added the energy equation. With the same similarity variable it becomes \theta&#x27;&#x27; + \tfrac12 Pr\,f\theta&#x27; = 0, which integrates in closed form once f is known, and the wall gradient is close to 0.332\,Pr^{1/3} for every Prandtl number of gases and ordinary liquids. That gives Nu_x = 0.332\,Re_x^{1/2}Pr^{1/3}, the starting point of every convection course (~engineeringkit.heat_transfer.flat_plate_nusselt). This example solves Blasius and Pohlhausen&#x27;s equations and then cools a plate whose boundary layer turns turbulent.">

    <div class="sphx-glr-thumbcontainer" tooltip="Allan Colburn at DuPont noticed in 1933 that turbulent heat transfer data collapse when the Stanton number is multiplied by Pr^{2/3}, and that this j factor equals half the friction factor. In 1934, with Thomas Chilton, he carried the same factor over to mass transfer: if St\,Pr^{2/3} = St_m Sc^{2/3}, a heat transfer coefficient gives the mass transfer coefficient, h_m = h/(\rho c_p Le^{2/3}) (~engineeringkit.heat_transfer.chilton_colburn_mass_transfer). It lets one experiment stand for three. This example checks the analogy on the laminar flat plate and uses it to explain the wet-bulb thermometer.">

    <div class="sphx-glr-thumbcontainer" tooltip="Boilers, air heaters and the cores of most shell-and-tube exchangers are banks of tubes in crossflow. The first rows see the free stream; deeper rows sit in the wakes of the ones ahead, whose turbulence raises their heat transfer. Algirdas Žukauskas, at the Lithuanian Academy of Sciences in Kaunas, gathered decades of his laboratory&#x27;s measurements on aligned and staggered banks into one form, \bar{Nu} = C_2 C Re_{max}^m Pr^{0.36} (Pr/Pr_s)^{1/4}, with constants for each layout and Reynolds range and a correction C_2 for banks of fewer than 20 rows (~engineeringkit.heat_transfer.zukauskas_nusselt, with tube_bank_max_velocity). This example sizes an air heater with it and compares the two layouts.">

In 1977 Stuart Churchill and M. Bernstein, applying the method of blending asymptotes that Churchill had used for free convection, wrote a single expression from creeping flow past the drag crisis (~engineeringkit.heat_transfer.churchill_bernstein_nusselt). This example compares it with Hilpert&#x27;s table and calibrates a hot-wire anemometer with it.">
