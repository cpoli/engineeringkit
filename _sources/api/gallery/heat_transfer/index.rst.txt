Examples
========

.. include:: /_generated/nav/heat_transfer.rst

This gallery walks through every public feature of
``engineeringkit.heat_transfer``, one breakthrough per script: Newton's
cooling and the convection correlations, transient conduction from Biot to
Heisler, Fourier's law, fins and finite differences, blackbody radiation and
view factors and the radiation network, phase change, and heat exchangers.

:doc:`Units, assumptions and numerical validation </guides/heat_transfer>`
gives the physical validity limits. Each script is self-contained and can be
run directly with ``python examples/heat_transfer/<section>/<script>.py``.

Sections
--------

- **convection** -- Newton's law of cooling, the Dittus-Boelter and Churchill-Chu correlations, Graetz's tube flow, Pohlhausen's flat plate, the Chilton-Colburn analogy, Žukauskas's tube banks and Churchill and Bernstein's cylinder.
- **transient** -- Biot's number, Kelvin's age of the Earth and Heisler's charts.
- **conduction** -- Fourier's law, Harper and Brown's fins and the Crank-Nicolson scheme.
- **radiation** -- Kirchhoff's law, Stefan-Boltzmann, Wien's displacement, Nusselt's view factors and Oppenheim's network.
- **phase_change** -- Stefan's ice problem, Nusselt's film condensation, the boiling curve and Rohsenow's nucleate boiling.
- **exchangers** -- Kays and London's effectiveness-NTU method and Bowman, Mueller and Nagle's correction factor.

    <div class="sphx-glr-thumbcontainer" tooltip="Joseph Fourier&#x27;s 1822 treatise made the heat flux proportional to the temperature gradient, q = -k\,\mathrm{d}T/\mathrm{d}x, derived the heat equation from it, and invented the series that bear his name to solve it. For steady flow through a slab the law gives \dot Q = kA\Delta T/L (~engineeringkit.heat_transfer.fourier_heat_rate), so a slab behaves like an electrical resistor L/(kA) (~engineeringkit.heat_transfer.plane_wall_resistance) and layered walls add like resistors in series (~engineeringkit.heat_transfer.composite_wall). Curved walls give logarithmic resistances, and with them the surprising critical radius of insulation. Where the heat spreads in two dimensions, the solution of Fourier&#x27;s equation is packed into a conduction shape factor S, with \dot Q = Sk\Delta T.">

    <div class="sphx-glr-thumbcontainer" tooltip="In 1701 Newton published, anonymously, a temperature scale reaching far beyond the melting point of tin. He heated a block of iron red hot, set it in a steady wind, and timed how long it took to cool. Assuming that the heat lost is proportional to the excess temperature, \dot Q = hA(T - T_\infty) (~engineeringkit.heat_transfer.convection_heat_rate), equal intervals of time should multiply the excess temperature by equal factors (~engineeringkit.heat_transfer.lumped_temperature). Newton then read temperatures off the clock. This example repeats the measurement on a model block, recovers the time constant from the readings, and checks the energy balance.">

    <div class="sphx-glr-thumbcontainer" tooltip="In 1891 Josef Stefan asked how fast sea ice thickens in the polar winter. The ice conducts heat from the freezing water below to the cold air above, and every millimetre of new ice releases latent heat at the moving interface, so the boundary is part of the unknown.     <div class="sphx-glr-thumbcontainer" tooltip="In 1859 Gustav Kirchhoff, explaining the dark lines that Fraunhofer had seen in the solar spectrum, proved from the second law that at every wavelength a body&#x27;s emissivity equals its absorptivity. A good absorber is a good emitter, and the perfect absorber, the blackbody, is the universal reference whose emission depends on temperature alone. For a gray surface in large surroundings the net exchange is \varepsilon\sigma A(T_s^4 - T_{sur}^4) (~engineeringkit.heat_transfer.radiation_heat_rate), often linearised as a coefficient h_r (~engineeringkit.heat_transfer.radiation_coefficient). This example shows why \alpha = \varepsilon is forced: anything else would let heat flow from cold to hot.">

    <div class="sphx-glr-thumbcontainer" tooltip="In 1804 Jean-Baptiste Biot heated one end of iron bars and read thermometers set along them. He saw that the temperature profile is set by a contest between conduction along the bar and loss from its surface. He could not write a consistent differential equation for it; Fourier did in 1807. The ratio of the two effects, now the Biot number Bi = hL/k (~engineeringkit.heat_transfer.biot_number), decides whether a body can be treated as a single temperature.
