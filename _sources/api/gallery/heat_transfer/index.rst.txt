Examples
========

.. include:: /_generated/nav/heat_transfer.rst

This gallery walks through every public feature of
``engineeringkit.heat_transfer``, one breakthrough per script: Newton's
cooling and the convection correlations, transient conduction from Biot to
Heisler, Fourier's law, fins and finite differences, blackbody radiation and
view factors, phase change, and heat exchangers.

:doc:`Units, assumptions and numerical validation </guides/heat_transfer>`
gives the physical validity limits. Each script is self-contained and can be
run directly with ``python examples/heat_transfer/<section>/<script>.py``.

Sections
--------

- **convection** -- Newton's law of cooling, the Dittus-Boelter and Churchill-Chu correlations.
- **transient** -- Biot's number, Kelvin's age of the Earth and Heisler's charts.
- **conduction** -- Fourier's law, Harper and Brown's fins and the Crank-Nicolson scheme.
- **radiation** -- Kirchhoff's law, Stefan-Boltzmann, Wien's displacement and Nusselt's view factors.
- **phase_change** -- Stefan's ice problem, Nusselt's film condensation and the boiling curve.
- **exchangers** -- Kays and London's effectiveness-NTU method.

.. raw:: html

    <div class="sphx-glr-thumbcontainer" tooltip="Joseph Fourier&#x27;s 1822 treatise made the heat flux proportional to the temperature gradient, q = -k\,\mathrm{d}T/\mathrm{d}x, derived the heat equation from it, and invented the series that bear his name to solve it. For steady flow through a slab the law gives \dot Q = kA\Delta T/L (~engineeringkit.heat_transfer.fourier_heat_rate), so a slab behaves like an electrical resistor L/(kA) (~engineeringkit.heat_transfer.plane_wall_resistance) and layered walls add like resistors in series (~engineeringkit.heat_transfer.composite_wall). Curved walls give logarithmic resistances, and with them the surprising critical radius of insulation.">

.. raw:: html

    <div class="sphx-glr-thumbcontainer" tooltip="In 1701 Newton published, anonymously, a temperature scale reaching far beyond the melting point of tin. He heated a block of iron red hot, set it in a steady wind, and timed how long it took to cool. Assuming that the heat lost is proportional to the excess temperature, \dot Q = hA(T - T_\infty) (~engineeringkit.heat_transfer.convection_heat_rate), equal intervals of time should multiply the excess temperature by equal factors (~engineeringkit.heat_transfer.lumped_temperature). Newton then read temperatures off the clock. This example repeats the measurement on a model block, recovers the time constant from the readings, and checks the energy balance.">

In McAdams&#x27;s rounded form, Nu = 0.023\,Re^{0.8}Pr^{n} with n = 0.4 for heating and 0.3 for cooling (~engineeringkit.heat_transfer.dittus_boelter_nusselt), it became the most quoted equation in convection.     <div class="sphx-glr-thumbcontainer" tooltip="In 1891 Josef Stefan asked how fast sea ice thickens in the polar winter. The ice conducts heat from the freezing water below to the cold air above, and every millimetre of new ice releases latent heat at the moving interface, so the boundary is part of the unknown. Franz Neumann had found the exact similarity solution in his lectures of the 1860s: the front advances as s = 2\lambda\sqrt{\alpha t} with \lambda e^{\lambda^2}\operatorname{erf}\lambda = Ste/\sqrt\pi (~engineeringkit.heat_transfer.neumann_stefan_front, stefan_number). This example grows a winter of sea ice, checks the energy balance at the front, and compares with Stefan&#x27;s own quasi-steady estimate.">

Radiation
---------

Blackbody laws, gray-surface exchange and view factors.


.. raw:: html

  <div id='sg-tag-list' class='sphx-glr-tag-list'></div>


.. raw:: html

    <div class="sphx-glr-thumbnails">

.. raw:: html

    <div class="sphx-glr-thumbcontainer" tooltip="In 1859 Gustav Kirchhoff, explaining the dark lines that Fraunhofer had seen in the solar spectrum, proved from the second law that at every wavelength a body&#x27;s emissivity equals its absorptivity. A good absorber is a good emitter, and the perfect absorber, the blackbody, is the universal reference whose emission depends on temperature alone. For a gray surface in large surroundings the net exchange is \varepsilon\sigma A(T_s^4 - T_{sur}^4) (~engineeringkit.heat_transfer.radiation_heat_rate), often linearised as a coefficient h_r (~engineeringkit.heat_transfer.radiation_coefficient). This example shows why \alpha = \varepsilon is forced: anything else would let heat flow from cold to hot.">

    <div class="sphx-glr-thumbcontainer" tooltip="In 1879 Josef Stefan reread John Tyndall&#x27;s measurements of a glowing platinum wire: at 1200 °C it radiated 11.7 times as much as at 525 °C. The ratio of the absolute temperatures to the fourth power is 11.6, and Stefan proposed E \propto T^4. In 1884 his former student Ludwig Boltzmann derived the law from thermodynamics and Maxwell&#x27;s radiation pressure, E_b = \sigma T^4 (~engineeringkit.heat_transfer.stefan_boltzmann_emissive_power). The constant \sigma was explained only by Planck&#x27;s spectrum (~engineeringkit.heat_transfer.planck_spectral_emissive_power), whose integral this example checks against the law.">

Doppler shifts and the second law then forced the spectrum to keep its shape as the temperature changes: the spectral emissive power divided by T^5 depends only on \lambda T. Its peak therefore sits at \lambda_{max}T = b (~engineeringkit.heat_transfer.blackbody_peak_wavelength), with b = 2898 um K. The law says why the Sun peaks in the visible, a lamp filament in the near infrared and a person at about 10 um. This example finds the peak of Planck&#x27;s spectrum numerically and checks the similarity that Wien proved.">

In 1928 Wilhelm Nusselt found a construction that a draughtsman could do: project the second surface onto a unit hemisphere centred on a point of the first, then project that image straight down onto the base circle. The fraction of the circle covered is the view factor from the point. Picking points uniformly in the circle is the same as picking cosine-weighted directions, which makes the construction a Monte Carlo method. This example uses it to check the closed forms for opposed rectangles (~engineeringkit.heat_transfer.view_factor_parallel_plates) and coaxial disks (~engineeringkit.heat_transfer.view_factor_coaxial_disks).">

Transient conduction
--------------------

Lumped bodies, the semi-infinite solid and the plane wall.


.. raw:: html

.. raw:: html

    <div class="sphx-glr-thumbcontainer" tooltip="In 1804 Jean-Baptiste Biot heated one end of iron bars and read thermometers set along them. He saw that the temperature profile is set by a contest between conduction along the bar and loss from its surface. He could not write a consistent differential equation for it; Fourier did in 1807. The ratio of the two effects, now the Biot number Bi = hL/k (~engineeringkit.heat_transfer.biot_number), decides whether a body can be treated as a single temperature. This example cools a plane wall at three Biot numbers and compares the lumped model e^{-Bi\,Fo} with the exact series solution.">

    <div class="sphx-glr-thumbcontainer" tooltip="In 1862 William Thomson, later Lord Kelvin, treated the Earth as a semi-infinite solid that began at a uniform molten temperature and has cooled from its surface ever since. The temperature below the surface is then T_s + (T_i - T_s)\operatorname{erf}(x/2\sqrt{\alpha t}) (~engineeringkit.heat_transfer.semi_infinite_temperature), and the surface gradient (T_i - T_s)/\sqrt{\pi\alpha t} falls with time. From the gradient measured in mines, about 1 °F per 50 ft, he found an age of about 98 million years. This example redoes his arithmetic in SI units and checks the gradient against the profile itself.">
