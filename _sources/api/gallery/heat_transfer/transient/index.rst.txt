Transient conduction
--------------------

Lumped bodies, the semi-infinite solid and the plane wall.

.. raw:: html

    <div class="sphx-glr-thumbcontainer" tooltip="In 1804 Jean-Baptiste Biot heated one end of iron bars and read thermometers set along them. He saw that the temperature profile is set by a contest between conduction along the bar and loss from its surface. He could not write a consistent differential equation for it; Fourier did in 1807. The ratio of the two effects, now the Biot number Bi = hL/k (~engineeringkit.heat_transfer.biot_number), decides whether a body can be treated as a single temperature. This example cools a plane wall at three Biot numbers and compares the lumped model e^{-Bi\,Fo} with the exact series solution.">

    <div class="sphx-glr-thumbcontainer" tooltip="In 1862 William Thomson, later Lord Kelvin, treated the Earth as a semi-infinite solid that began at a uniform molten temperature and has cooled from its surface ever since. The temperature below the surface is then T_s + (T_i - T_s)\operatorname{erf}(x/2\sqrt{\alpha t}) (~engineeringkit.heat_transfer.semi_infinite_temperature), and the surface gradient (T_i - T_s)/\sqrt{\pi\alpha t} falls with time. From the gradient measured in mines, about 1 °F per 50 ft, he found an age of about 98 million years. This example redoes his arithmetic in SI units and checks the gradient against the profile itself.">

Before computers, nobody summed it by hand. In 1947 M. P. Heisler, working on induction heating, noticed that after a short time only the first term matters, and plotted that term as charts of centre temperature against the Fourier number for each Biot number. Generations of students read them with a ruler. plane_wall_transient sums the series (or its first term, terms=1); this example redraws the centre chart, measures the one-term error, and times the quench of a steel plate.">
