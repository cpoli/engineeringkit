Examples
========

.. include:: /_generated/nav/mechanical_engineering.rst

This gallery walks through every public feature of
``engineeringkit.mechanical_engineering``, one breakthrough per script:
rigid-body dynamics, gears and belts, screws, bearings, bolts and springs,
linkages and robot arms, vibration, normal modes and absorbers, and the whirling
and balanced rotor.

:doc:`Units, assumptions and numerical validation </guides/mechanical_engineering>`
gives the physical validity limits. Each script is self-contained and can be
run directly with ``python examples/mechanical_engineering/<section>/<script>.py``.

Sections
--------

- **dynamics** -- Huygens's moment of inertia, Coriolis's work and kinetic energy and Lagrange's equations.
- **transmission** -- Euler's involute gear, the Euler-Eytelwein belt-friction law and Lewis's gear-tooth bending.
- **machine_elements** -- Coulomb's power screw, Petroff's and Sommerfeld's journal bearings, the L10 life of rolling bearings,
  Rötscher's bolted-joint diagram and Wahl's spring factor.
- **mechanisms** -- Reuleaux's slider-crank, Grashof's condition, Freudenstein's synthesis, Denavit-Hartenberg kinematics and
  Aronhold and Kennedy's instant centres.
- **vibration** -- Rayleigh's free and forced vibration, Frahm's absorber, Holzer's torsional method, Bernoulli's normal modes and
  Dunkerley's whirling shafts.
- **rotordynamics** -- the Jeffcott rotor and its critical speed, and Dalby's balancing of rotating masses.

    <div class="sphx-glr-thumbcontainer" tooltip="Franz Reuleaux&#x27;s Theoretische Kinematik (1875) treated every machine as a kinematic chain of rigid links joined by pairs (turning, sliding, screw), with one link held fixed. Fixing different links of the same chain gives different machines, which he called inversions. Grounding the cylinder of the slider-crank chain gives the piston engine, grounding the crank gives the Whitworth quick-return, and grounding the rod gives the oscillating cylinder. For the engine, the piston position x = r\cos\theta + \sqrt{l^2 - r^2\sin^2\theta} (~engineeringkit.mechanical_engineering.slider_crank_displacement) is not harmonic. Its acceleration at top dead centre is r\omega^2(1 + r/l) but only r\omega^2(1 - r/l) at bottom dead centre, which is the source of an engine&#x27;s second-order shaking force.">

    <div class="sphx-glr-thumbcontainer" tooltip="Rankine (1869) had argued that a shaft could not run stably above its whirling speed, yet de Laval&#x27;s steam turbines of the 1880s and 1890s did exactly that. Henry Jeffcott (1919) explained why with the simplest model: a disk whose centre of mass sits a small distance e from a flexible massless shaft. The shaft centre whirls in step with the spin at radius e r^2/\sqrt{(1 - r^2)^2 + (2\zeta r)^2}, with r the speed over the critical speed \sqrt{k/m} (~engineeringkit.mechanical_engineering.jeffcott_response). Through resonance the whirl swings round by half a turn. Far above it the heavy side of the disk faces inward and the disk spins about its own centre of mass, so the whirl radius settles at e. The force that reaches the bearings is the unbalance force (~engineeringkit.mechanical_engineering.rotating_unbalance_force) times the transmissibility (~engineeringkit.mechanical_engineering.transmissibility).">

    <div class="sphx-glr-thumbcontainer" tooltip="Two gears turn at a constant speed ratio only if the common normal at their point of contact always passes through one fixed point on the line of centres, the pitch point. Leonhard Euler (1765) showed that the involute of a circle, the curve traced by the end of a string unwound from it, meets this condition. Its normal is always tangent to the base circle, so the contact normal of two involutes is the fixed line tangent to both base circles. The ratio is then the ratio of base radii, that is of tooth counts (~engineeringkit.mechanical_engineering.gear_ratio), and it does not change if the centres are pulled apart. The example checks both properties and the power balance of a gear train (~engineeringkit.mechanical_engineering.gear_output_speed, gear_output_torque).">

    <div class="sphx-glr-thumbcontainer" tooltip="Lord Rayleigh&#x27;s The Theory of Sound (1877) treated every vibrating system, from strings to bells, as a set of linear oscillators. The simplest is a mass on a spring with a dashpot, m\ddot x + c\dot x + kx = 0. It rings at \omega_n = \sqrt{k/m} (~engineeringkit.mechanical_engineering.natural_frequency), and damping lowers the frequency to \omega_n\sqrt{1 - \zeta^2} (~engineeringkit.mechanical_engineering.damped_frequency, damping_ratio). Rayleigh also showed how to estimate a frequency from energy alone: equate the peak kinetic and potential energies for an assumed shape. With a linear stretch profile, a spring&#x27;s own mass m_s adds m_s/3 to the moving mass. The example checks the simulated response (~engineeringkit.mechanical_engineering.simulate_sdof) and Rayleigh&#x27;s correction against the exact solution.">

    <div class="sphx-glr-thumbcontainer" tooltip="Hermann Frahm (patent filed 1909) stopped ships&#x27; hulls and machines from vibrating at their running speed by attaching a small spring-mass system tuned to that speed. At the tuned frequency the absorber swings so that its spring force cancels the applied force, and the machine stands still. The cure works at one frequency only: the system now has two resonances, one on each side. Jacob Pieter Den Hartog and John Ormondroyd (1928) added damping to the absorber. Every response curve then passes through two fixed points, whatever the damping, and the best design tunes the absorber to make them equal and puts the peaks there (~engineeringkit.mechanical_engineering.tuned_mass_damper). This is the tuned mass damper of tall buildings.">
