    <div class="sphx-glr-thumbcontainer" tooltip="Charles-Augustin de Coulomb&#x27;s prize memoir Théorie des machines simples (1781, published 1785) measured sliding friction and found it proportional to the normal load and nearly independent of contact area and speed: F = \mu N. A screw thread is an inclined plane wrapped round a cylinder. Unwrapping one turn gives a slope of lead angle \tan\lambda = L/(\pi d_m), and Coulomb&#x27;s law on that slope gives the torque to raise a load, T = \tfrac12 W d_m\tan(\phi + \lambda) with \tan\phi = \mu (~engineeringkit.mechanical_engineering.screw_torque_raise). The example solves the force balance on the unwrapped thread directly and shows why a self-locking screw is always less than 50% efficient.">

    <div class="sphx-glr-thumbcontainer" tooltip="Railway axle bearings of the 1880s ran on oil, and their friction did not behave like Coulomb&#x27;s: it grew with speed and with the oil&#x27;s viscosity, and it fell as the load rose. Nikolai Petroff (1883) explained this by treating the oil film as a thin layer sheared between the journal and its sleeve. With the journal centred, the shear stress is \mu\omega r/c all round, so the friction torque is T = 2\pi\mu\omega r^3 L/c (~engineeringkit.mechanical_engineering.petroff_friction_torque) and the friction coefficient follows f = 2\pi^2 (\mu N/P)(r/c). The example checks Petroff&#x27;s thin-film torque against the exact flow between concentric cylinders.">

    <div class="sphx-glr-thumbcontainer" tooltip="Identical ball bearings run under identical loads fail at very different times, because fatigue cracks start at randomly placed weak points below the raceway. Gustaf Lundberg and Arvid Palmgren (1947) of SKF treated the lives as Weibull-distributed, with a slope of about 10/9 for ball bearings. They defined the rating life L_{10}, which 90% of bearings reach, and showed that it varies as (C/P)^p with the basic load rating C and the applied load P. The exponent is p = 3 for balls and 10/3 for rollers (~engineeringkit.mechanical_engineering.bearing_l10_life). The example draws a simulated batch of bearings and checks both statements.">

    <div class="sphx-glr-thumbcontainer" tooltip="Nineteenth-century designers sized a bolt by dividing the load by its root area. Friedrich Rötscher&#x27;s Die Maschinenelemente (1927) drew the joint diagram that showed why a preloaded bolt does far better: bolt and clamped members are two springs, one stretched and one squeezed by the preload F_i, and an external load P stretches the bolt further only by as much as it relieves the members. The bolt sees only the fraction C = k_b/(k_b + k_m) of the load (~engineeringkit.mechanical_engineering.bolted_joint) until the members separate. The members&#x27; stiffness comes from cones of compressed material spreading from under the head and nut (~engineeringkit.mechanical_engineering.member_stiffness), the bolt&#x27;s from its shank and threads in series (~engineeringkit.mechanical_engineering.bolt_stiffness). Rötscher&#x27;s 1927 book could not be consulted; the date and attribution follow J. H. Bickford, An Introduction to the Design and Behavior of Bolted Joints, and the Bolt Council&#x27;s history of joint design.">

    <div class="sphx-glr-thumbcontainer" tooltip="The wire of a helical spring is a bar in torsion, twisted by the torque FD/2, so the nominal shear stress is 8FD/\pi d^3 and the rate k = Gd^4/8D^3N (~engineeringkit.mechanical_engineering.spring_rate). Springs broke in fatigue at loads this predicted to be safe. Arthur Wahl, at Westinghouse, analysed the wire as a curved bar: on the inside of the coil the fibres are shorter, so the same twist strains them more, and the direct shear of the load adds on that side too. His factor K_W = (4C - 1)/(4C - 4) + 0.615/C (~engineeringkit.mechanical_engineering.wahl_factor), with C = D/d, multiplies the nominal stress (~engineeringkit.mechanical_engineering.spring_shear_stress). The 1929 paper is cited from Wahl&#x27;s Mechanical Springs (1944) and Shigley.">
