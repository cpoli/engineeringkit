    <div class="sphx-glr-thumbcontainer" tooltip="Two gears turn at a constant speed ratio only if the common normal at their point of contact always passes through one fixed point on the line of centres, the pitch point. Leonhard Euler (1765) showed that the involute of a circle, the curve traced by the end of a string unwound from it, meets this condition. Its normal is always tangent to the base circle, so the contact normal of two involutes is the fixed line tangent to both base circles. The ratio is then the ratio of base radii, that is of tooth counts (~engineeringkit.mechanical_engineering.gear_ratio), and it does not change if the centres are pulled apart. The example checks both properties and the power balance of a gear train (~engineeringkit.mechanical_engineering.gear_output_speed, gear_output_torque).">

    <div class="sphx-glr-thumbcontainer" tooltip="A sailor holds a heavy load with a few turns of rope round a bollard. Leonhard Euler (1762, published 1769) explained why: on each small arc d\theta of contact the rope presses on the drum with force T\,d\theta, friction can resist up to \mu T\,d\theta, so tension can grow by dT = \mu T\,d\theta and T_1/T_2 = e^{\mu\theta}. Johann Albert Eytelwein (1808) brought the result to engineers, for whom it sets the power a belt drive can transmit (~engineeringkit.mechanical_engineering.belt_tension_ratio, belt_speed). The example builds the exponential from small arcs, then sizes a flat and a V-belt.">

    <div class="sphx-glr-thumbcontainer" tooltip="Wilfred Lewis, of William Sellers and Co. in Philadelphia, gave the first rational strength calculation for gear teeth in a talk to the Engineers&#x27; Club of Philadelphia on 15 October 1892. He treated the tooth as a cantilever with the whole load at its tip and noticed that a parabola drawn from the load point, tangent to the tooth flanks at the root, is a beam of uniform strength. The root section where they touch is therefore the weakest, and the stress there is \sigma = W_t/(F m Y) (~engineeringkit.mechanical_engineering.lewis_bending_stress), with a form factor Y that depends only on the tooth shape and so on the number of teeth (~engineeringkit.mechanical_engineering.lewis_form_factor). Carl Barth&#x27;s factor raises the stress for the dynamic load at speed (~engineeringkit.mechanical_engineering.barth_velocity_factor). The AGMA bending equation still starts from Lewis&#x27;s.">
