    <div class="sphx-glr-thumbcontainer" tooltip="Lord Rayleigh&#x27;s The Theory of Sound (1877) treated every vibrating system, from strings to bells, as a set of linear oscillators. The simplest is a mass on a spring with a dashpot, m\ddot x + c\dot x + kx = 0. It rings at \omega_n = \sqrt{k/m} (~engineeringkit.mechanical_engineering.natural_frequency), and damping lowers the frequency to \omega_n\sqrt{1 - \zeta^2} (~engineeringkit.mechanical_engineering.damped_frequency, damping_ratio). Rayleigh also showed how to estimate a frequency from energy alone: equate the peak kinetic and potential energies for an assumed shape. With a linear stretch profile, a spring&#x27;s own mass m_s adds m_s/3 to the moving mass. The example checks the simulated response (~engineeringkit.mechanical_engineering.simulate_sdof) and Rayleigh&#x27;s correction against the exact solution.">

    <div class="sphx-glr-thumbcontainer" tooltip="Hermann Frahm (patent filed 1909) stopped ships&#x27; hulls and machines from vibrating at their running speed by attaching a small spring-mass system tuned to that speed. At the tuned frequency the absorber swings so that its spring force cancels the applied force, and the machine stands still. The cure works at one frequency only: the system now has two resonances, one on each side. Jacob Pieter Den Hartog and John Ormondroyd (1928) added damping to the absorber. Every response curve then passes through two fixed points, whatever the damping, and the best design tunes the absorber to make them equal and puts the peaks there (~engineeringkit.mechanical_engineering.tuned_mass_damper). This is the tuned mass damper of tall buildings.">

    <div class="sphx-glr-thumbcontainer" tooltip="Diesel engines, ship propellers and generators share long shafts that can twist resonantly, and a crankshaft that does so breaks. Heinrich Holzer (1921) found their natural frequencies with a table instead of a determinant. Guess a frequency, give the first disk unit twist, and march along the shaft. Each disk adds an inertia torque J_i\omega^2\theta_i, and each shaft twists by the accumulated torque over its stiffness. At a natural frequency the torque left over beyond the free end is zero (~engineeringkit.mechanical_engineering.holzer_residual, holzer_frequencies). The example checks Holzer&#x27;s frequencies against the eigenvalues of the stiffness and inertia matrices.">

    <div class="sphx-glr-thumbcontainer" tooltip="The third chapter of Rayleigh&#x27;s Theory of Sound drives the damped oscillator with a harmonic force, m\ddot x + c\dot x + kx = F_0\cos\omega t. Once the free motion has died away the mass moves at the forcing frequency with amplitude (F_0/k)\,M, the static deflection times the magnification factor (~engineeringkit.mechanical_engineering.magnification_factor), and lags the force by a phase (~engineeringkit.mechanical_engineering.phase_angle) that passes through 90 degrees at \omega = \omega_n whatever the damping. Below resonance the spring carries the force, at resonance only damping limits the motion, and above it the mass&#x27;s inertia does. The example compares a simulated run-up of three forcing frequencies with the closed form.">

    <div class="sphx-glr-thumbcontainer" tooltip="Daniel Bernoulli argued in 1753 that a vibrating string sounds many simple vibrations at once, each with its own shape and frequency, and that any motion is their sum. For masses joined by springs the simple vibrations are the solutions of the eigenproblem K\phi = \omega^2 M\phi (~engineeringkit.mechanical_engineering.normal_modes). The modes are orthogonal through M and K, so the equations M\ddot x + Kx = 0 split into independent oscillators, one per mode (~engineeringkit.mechanical_engineering.modal_response), and a harmonic force excites each mode in proportion to its share of the force (~engineeringkit.mechanical_engineering.harmonic_response). The example builds a three-storey shear frame and checks both superpositions against direct solutions.">

    <div class="sphx-glr-thumbcontainer" tooltip="Stanley Dunkerley measured the whirling speeds of shafts carrying pulleys at Owens College, Manchester, and found an empirical rule: the inverse squares of the critical speeds of each pulley alone, and of the bare shaft, add up to the inverse square of the critical speed of the whole, 1/\omega^2 \approx \sum_i 1/\omega_i^2 (~engineeringkit.mechanical_engineering.dunkerley_frequency). The sum is the trace of the flexibility-mass matrix, the sum of the inverse squares of every natural frequency, so it always underestimates the fundamental. Rayleigh&#x27;s quotient with the static deflection as the trial shape (~engineeringkit.mechanical_engineering.rayleigh_frequency) always overestimates it. The example brackets the first critical speed of a simply supported shaft with three disks.">
