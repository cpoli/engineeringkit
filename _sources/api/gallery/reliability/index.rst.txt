Examples
========

.. include:: /_generated/nav/reliability.rst

This gallery walks through every public feature of
``engineeringkit.reliability``, one breakthrough per script: lifetime
distributions and censored data, series, parallel and k-out-of-n systems,
stress-strength and first-order structural reliability, fault and event
trees, availability and replacement, and reliability growth.

:doc:`Units, assumptions and numerical validation </guides/reliability>`
gives the validity limits. Each script is self-contained and can be run
directly with ``python examples/reliability/<section>/<script>.py``.

Sections
--------

- **lifetimes** -- Halley's life table, Poisson's rare failures, Weibull's distribution, Epstein and Sobel's life testing and the Kaplan-Meier estimator.
- **systems** -- Von Neumann's redundancy, the AGREE report's series systems, k-out-of-n systems and Birnbaum's importance.
- **structural** -- Freudenthal's stress-strength interference and Hasofer and Lind's reliability index.
- **risk** -- Watson's fault tree, WASH-1400's event trees and IEC 61508's safety integrity levels.
- **maintenance** -- Barlow and Proschan's age replacement and Markov availability.
- **growth** -- Duane's reliability growth.

    <div class="sphx-glr-thumbcontainer" tooltip="James Duane, at General Electric&#x27;s Motor and Generator Department, plotted the failure records of five development programmes, from hydraulic devices to aircraft generators. On log-log paper the cumulative MTBF, total test time over total failures, rose along a straight line, each programme with a slope near 0.5. A straight line is a power law, \theta_c = K t^\alpha (~engineeringkit.reliability.reliability_growth_duane), so a few months of data predict how much more testing a reliability target needs. Because the failure count is N(t) = t^{1-\alpha}/K, the current failure rate is dN/dt, and the instantaneous MTBF is the cumulative one divided by 1 - \alpha.">

    <div class="sphx-glr-thumbcontainer" tooltip="In 1693 Edmond Halley used five years of births and burials from Breslau to tabulate how many of 1000 one-year-olds were still alive at each later age. The fall from one age to the next is the probability of dying in that year, the first estimate of what reliability engineers call the hazard rate (~engineeringkit.reliability.life_table_hazard). Halley&#x27;s hazard is high in infancy, lowest around age ten and rises steadily with age: the bathtub curve (~engineeringkit.reliability.bathtub_hazard) that describes many machines as well as people.">

The shape \beta sets the hazard (~engineeringkit.reliability.weibull_hazard): falling for \beta &lt; 1, constant at 1, rising above. Because \ln(-\ln R) = \beta \ln t - \beta \ln \eta, failure data plotted on those axes fall on a line whose slope is \beta. The example fits a small life test that way and compares the mean life with weibull_mttf.">

Epstein and Sobel (1953) showed that for exponential lives the total time on test T, summed over failed and surviving units, carries all the information: \hat\theta = T/r after r failures, and 2T/\theta is chi-square with 2r degrees of freedom. That gives exact confidence bounds (~engineeringkit.reliability.mtbf_confidence_bounds). The example stops a test of 12 units at the fourth failure, many times over, and checks that the 90% bounds catch the true MTBF 90% of the time.">

The example censors a Weibull sample at random and recovers the true survival curve.">

Maintenance
-----------

Repairable units, availability and when to replace a part before it fails.


.. raw:: html

  <div id='sg-tag-list' class='sphx-glr-tag-list'></div>


.. raw:: html

    <div class="sphx-glr-thumbnails">

.. thumbnail-parent-div-open

.. raw:: html

    <div class="sphx-glr-thumbcontainer" tooltip="Should a part be replaced on a schedule, before it fails? Richard Barlow and Frank Proschan answered with renewal theory. Replace at age T or at failure, whichever comes first. Each cycle then costs c_p R(T) + c_f F(T) and lasts \int_0^T R\,dt on average, so the long-run cost rate is their ratio (~engineeringkit.reliability.age_replacement_cost_rate). A finite best age exists only if the hazard rises (Weibull \beta &gt; 1) and a failure costs more than a planned replacement. With a constant hazard a new part is no better than the old one, so planned replacement only adds cost (~engineeringkit.reliability.renewal_interval_cost_rate).">

.. only:: html

  .. image:: /api/gallery/reliability/maintenance/images/thumb/sphx_glr_plot_01_barlow_proschan_replacement_thumb.png

.. raw:: html

      <div class="sphx-glr-thumbnail-title">Barlow and Proschan's age replacement</div>
    </div>


.. raw:: html

    <div class="sphx-glr-thumbcontainer" tooltip="A repairable unit fails and is mended over and over, so the question is not whether it survives but what fraction of time it is up. If failures and repairs both happen at constant rates, the unit is a continuous-time Markov chain. Barlow and Proschan&#x27;s 1965 monograph put such models at the centre of reliability theory. For one unit the up-probability decays from one to the steady state \mu/(\lambda + \mu) = \mathrm{MTBF}/(\mathrm{MTBF} + \mathrm{MTTR}) with time constant 1/(\lambda + \mu) (~engineeringkit.reliability.markov_availability). Larger systems need the matrix exponential of the transition-rate matrix (~engineeringkit.reliability.markov_state_probabilities).">

Risk

Fault trees, event trees and the safety integrity of protection systems.

.. raw:: html

    <div class="sphx-glr-thumbcontainer" tooltip="In 1961 the US Air Force asked Bell Telephone Laboratories whether the Minuteman launch-control system could fire a missile without a valid order. H. A. Watson&#x27;s answer was a new diagram, the fault tree. It starts from the undesired top event and works downward through AND gates (every input must happen) and OR gates (any input will do) until it reaches basic failures whose probabilities are known (~engineeringkit.reliability.fault_tree_probability). Boeing took the method into the rest of the Minuteman programme, and it spread to aviation and nuclear safety. One trap is an event that feeds more than one branch: the gate inputs are then dependent, and combining gates one by one gives the wrong answer.">

  .. image:: /api/gallery/reliability/risk/images/thumb/sphx_glr_plot_01_watson_fault_tree_thumb.png

.. raw:: html

    <div class="sphx-glr-thumbcontainer" tooltip="The Reactor Safety Study (WASH-1400, 1975), led by Norman Rasmussen, was the first attempt to put a number on the risk of a nuclear power plant. Its method worked forward from each initiating event, such as a pipe break, and asked in turn whether each safety system worked. Every path through the resulting event tree is an accident sequence. Its frequency is the initiator&#x27;s frequency times the branch probabilities (~engineeringkit.reliability.event_tree_probability). Fault trees supplied those branch probabilities. The study was criticised for its uncertainty bounds, but the Three Mile Island accident of 1979 followed a small-break sequence of the kind it had ranked among the most likely, and probabilistic risk assessment became standard practice.">

    <div class="sphx-glr-thumbcontainer" tooltip="Programmable electronics took over process trips, railway signalling and machine guards in the 1980s, and nobody could say how safe a software-based protection system was. The International Electrotechnical Commission&#x27;s answer, IEC 61508 (first parts published 1998), grades each safety function by the risk reduction it must achieve. For a function that acts only on rare demands, the measure is its average probability of failure on demand. A dangerous fault that no self-test detects stays hidden until the next proof test, so the channel&#x27;s unavailability rises from zero after each test, and over an interval T it averages \lambda_{DU} T/2 (~engineeringkit.reliability.safety_integrity_pfd). Each decade of PFDavg is one safety integrity level (~engineeringkit.reliability.safety_integrity_level).">

Structural reliability
----------------------

The probability that a load exceeds a resistance.


.. raw:: html

  <div id='sg-tag-list' class='sphx-glr-tag-list'></div>


.. raw:: html

    <div class="sphx-glr-thumbnails">

.. thumbnail-parent-div-open

.. raw:: html

    <div class="sphx-glr-thumbcontainer" tooltip="Alfred Freudenthal (1947) argued that a safety factor, the ratio of mean strength to mean load, says nothing about safety on its own: two designs with the same factor can fail at very different rates if their loads and strengths scatter differently. What matters is the overlap of the two distributions. For independent normal strength S and stress s, R = P(S &gt; s) = \Phi(\beta) with \beta = (\mu_S - \mu_s)/\sqrt{\sigma_S^2 + \sigma_s^2} (~engineeringkit.reliability.stress_strength_reliability).">

  .. image:: /api/gallery/reliability/structural/images/thumb/sphx_glr_plot_01_freudenthal_interference_thumb.png

.. raw:: html

    <div class="sphx-glr-thumbcontainer" tooltip="Cornell&#x27;s 1969 index, mean over standard deviation of the safety margin g, has a flaw: it changes when the same failure condition is written differently, say R - S &lt; 0 or R/S - 1 &lt; 0. Hasofer and Lind (1974) fixed it by measuring, in standardised variables, the shortest distance from the mean to the failure surface itself (~engineeringkit.reliability.hasofer_lind_index). The nearest point, the design point, is where failure is most likely to happen, and p_f \approx \Phi(-\beta).">

Systems

How the reliability of a system follows from its parts and their arrangement.

    <div class="sphx-glr-thumbcontainer" tooltip="John von Neumann&#x27;s 1956 lectures asked how a computer, or a brain, can work when every component is unreliable. Two answers came out. Active parallel redundancy, 1 - (1 - r)^n (~engineeringkit.reliability.parallel_reliability), survives if any copy survives. For logic, where a wrong output is as bad as none, he used majority voting: three copies and a voter give 3r^2 - 2r^3, and voting on voters drives reliability toward one, but only if each part is better than a coin toss, r &gt; 1/2.">

    <div class="sphx-glr-thumbcontainer" tooltip="In the early 1950s, most of the US Navy&#x27;s electronics was out of action at any given time, largely because of vacuum-tube failures. The Advisory Group on Reliability of Electronic Equipment (AGREE) reported in 1957 that reliability must be specified as a number, predicted and demonstrated by test. Its working model was a series system of parts with constant failure rates: the system&#x27;s hazard (~engineeringkit.reliability.exponential_hazard) is the sum of the parts&#x27; rates and its MTBF the reciprocal. More parts means less reliability, R_s = \prod R_i (~engineeringkit.reliability.series_reliability).">
