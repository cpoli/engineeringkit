Examples
========

.. include:: /_generated/nav/reliability.rst

This gallery walks through every public feature of
``engineeringkit.reliability``, one breakthrough per script: lifetime
distributions and censored data, Weibull fitting and Bayesian failure
rates, accelerated life testing, series, parallel, k-out-of-n and network
systems, stress-strength, first-order and Monte Carlo structural
reliability, FMEA, fault and event trees, availability and replacement,
and reliability growth.

:doc:`Units, assumptions and numerical validation </guides/reliability>`
gives the validity limits. Each script is self-contained and can be run
directly with ``python examples/reliability/<section>/<script>.py``.

Sections
--------

- **lifetimes** -- Halley's life table, Poisson's rare failures, Weibull's distribution, Epstein and Sobel's life testing, the Kaplan-Meier estimator and McAlister's lognormal law.
- **life_data** -- Johnson's median ranks, Cohen's censored maximum likelihood and Raiffa and Schlaifer's conjugate prior for a failure rate.
- **acceleration** -- Arrhenius's law and Eyring's activated complex.
- **systems** -- Von Neumann's redundancy, the AGREE report's series systems, k-out-of-n systems, Birnbaum's importance and Esary and Proschan's path and cut bounds.
- **structural** -- Freudenthal's stress-strength interference, Hasofer and Lind's reliability index and Metropolis and Ulam's Monte Carlo method.
- **risk** -- MIL-P-1629's FMEA, Watson's fault tree, WASH-1400's event trees and IEC 61508's safety integrity levels.
- **maintenance** -- Barlow and Proschan's age replacement and Markov availability.
- **growth** -- Duane's reliability growth.

    <div class="sphx-glr-thumbcontainer" tooltip="James Duane, at General Electric&#x27;s Motor and Generator Department, plotted the failure records of five development programmes, from hydraulic devices to aircraft generators. On log-log paper the cumulative MTBF, total test time over total failures, rose along a straight line, each programme with a slope near 0.5. Maintenance

Repairable units, availability and when to replace a part before it fails.


.. raw:: html

.. raw:: html

    <div class="sphx-glr-thumbcontainer" tooltip="Should a part be replaced on a schedule, before it fails? Richard Barlow and Frank Proschan answered with renewal theory. Replace at age T or at failure, whichever comes first. Each cycle then costs c_p R(T) + c_f F(T) and lasts \int_0^T R\,dt on average, so the long-run cost rate is their ratio (~engineeringkit.reliability.age_replacement_cost_rate). A finite best age exists only if the hazard rises (Weibull \beta &gt; 1) and a failure costs more than a planned replacement. With a constant hazard a new part is no better than the old one, so planned replacement only adds cost (~engineeringkit.reliability.renewal_interval_cost_rate).">

    <div class="sphx-glr-thumbcontainer" tooltip="In 1961 the US Air Force asked Bell Telephone Laboratories whether the Minuteman launch-control system could fire a missile without a valid order. H. A. Watson&#x27;s answer was a new diagram, the fault tree. It starts from the undesired top event and works downward through AND gates (every input must happen) and OR gates (any input will do) until it reaches basic failures whose probabilities are known (~engineeringkit.reliability.fault_tree_probability). Structural reliability
----------------------

The probability that a load exceeds a resistance.


.. raw:: html

  <div id='sg-tag-list' class='sphx-glr-tag-list'></div>

    <div class="sphx-glr-thumbcontainer" tooltip="Alfred Freudenthal (1947) argued that a safety factor, the ratio of mean strength to mean load, says nothing about safety on its own: two designs with the same factor can fail at very different rates if their loads and strengths scatter differently. What matters is the overlap of the two distributions. For independent normal strength S and stress s, R = P(S &gt; s) = \Phi(\beta) with \beta = (\mu_S - \mu_s)/\sqrt{\sigma_S^2 + \sigma_s^2} (~engineeringkit.reliability.stress_strength_reliability).">

Hasofer and Lind (1974) fixed it by measuring, in standardised variables, the shortest distance from the mean to the failure surface itself (~engineeringkit.reliability.hasofer_lind_index). The nearest point, the design point, is where failure is most likely to happen, and p_f \approx \Phi(-\beta).">

With Nicholas Metropolis (1949) he described the Monte Carlo method: estimate a probability or an integral by random sampling. For a structure the failure probability P(g(X) &lt; 0) is the fraction of sampled load and resistance pairs that fail (~engineeringkit.reliability.monte_carlo_failure_probability). Systems
-------

How the reliability of a system follows from its parts and their arrangement.


.. raw:: html

  <div id='sg-tag-list' class='sphx-glr-tag-list'></div>


.. raw:: html

    <div class="sphx-glr-thumbnails">

.. thumbnail-parent-div-open

.. raw:: html

    <div class="sphx-glr-thumbcontainer" tooltip="John von Neumann&#x27;s 1956 lectures asked how a computer, or a brain, can work when every component is unreliable. Two answers came out. Active parallel redundancy, 1 - (1 - r)^n (~engineeringkit.reliability.parallel_reliability), survives if any copy survives. For logic, where a wrong output is as bad as none, he used majority voting: three copies and a voter give 3r^2 - 2r^3, and voting on voters drives reliability toward one, but only if each part is better than a coin toss, r &gt; 1/2.">
