    <div class="sphx-glr-thumbcontainer" tooltip="Albert Michelson&#x27;s harmonic analyser (1898) summed 80 Fourier terms mechanically, and its curves for a square wave overshot at every jump. Michelson suspected the machine. Josiah Willard Gibbs (1899) showed in Nature that the mathematics itself overshoots: near a discontinuity the partial sums (~engineeringkit.signal_processing.fourier_partial_sum) rise about 9% of the jump above it, however many terms are kept. The overshoot only narrows toward the jump. Henry Wilbraham had found the same effect in 1848, unnoticed.">

    <div class="sphx-glr-thumbcontainer" tooltip="A direct DFT of N samples needs N^2 complex multiplications. James Cooley and John Tukey (1965) split it into the DFTs of the even- and odd-numbered samples, combined by &quot;butterflies&quot; E_k \pm W^k O_k, and repeated the split down to single samples (~engineeringkit.signal_processing.radix2_fft). The cost falls to (N/2)\log_2 N. Gauss had used the same idea in 1805 to interpolate asteroid orbits. In 1965 it made spectral analysis on a computer routine, and it is what dft (NumPy&#x27;s FFT) does.">

    <div class="sphx-glr-thumbcontainer" tooltip="In his third communication on integral equations (1905), David Hilbert paired a function on the unit circle with its conjugate, the boundary values of the real and imaginary parts of one analytic function, through the singular kernel \cot\frac{s - t}{2}. In frequency terms the pairing shifts every component by -90°: \cos \to \sin. Dennis Gabor (1946) added the conjugate to a real signal as its imaginary part, z = x + j\mathcal{H}x, and called the result the analytic signal: it has no negative frequencies, its magnitude is the envelope and its phase advances at the instantaneous frequency (~engineeringkit.signal_processing.hilbert_transform, analytic_signal, envelope, instantaneous_frequency, hilbert_fir). The 1905 date and pages come from the Göttingen Nachrichten catalogue record; that the transform pair appears there follows F. W. King, Hilbert Transforms (Cambridge, 2009), a secondary source.">

    <div class="sphx-glr-thumbcontainer" tooltip="The FFT multiplies spectra cheaply, but the product of two DFTs is the circular convolution: the tail of the output wraps round onto its start. Thomas Stockham, at MIT&#x27;s Project MAC, showed at the 1966 Spring Joint Computer Conference how to get the linear convolution anyway, and fast: zero-pad both sequences to at least N_x + N_h - 1 samples, and, for a long record, cut it into blocks, convolve each through the FFT and add the overlapping tails, overlap-add (~engineeringkit.signal_processing.circular_convolution, overlap_add). Zero padding does not add resolution to a spectrum; it only samples the same spectrum more finely, which is the last section here.">

    <div class="sphx-glr-thumbcontainer" tooltip="The best basis for compressing a random signal, the Karhunen-Loève transform, depends on the signal&#x27;s covariance and has no fast algorithm. Nasir Ahmed, T. Natarajan and K. R. Rao (1974) proposed a fixed basis of sampled cosines that comes very close to it for strongly correlated signals. Because the DCT implicitly extends the record by its mirror image, there is no jump at the ends and the coefficients of a smooth signal decay fast; the DFT&#x27;s periodic extension has a jump and leaks. The DCT became the core of JPEG, MPEG and MP3 (~engineeringkit.signal_processing.dct, idct).">
