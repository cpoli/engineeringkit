Examples
========

.. include:: /_generated/nav/signal_processing.rst

This gallery walks through every public feature of
``engineeringkit.signal_processing``, one breakthrough per script: Fourier
series and the FFT, spectral estimation, sampling and quantization,
digital filter design, optimal and adaptive filters, and wavelets.

:doc:`Units, assumptions and numerical validation </guides/signal_processing>`
gives the validity limits. Each script is self-contained and can be run
directly with ``python examples/signal_processing/<section>/<script>.py``.

Sections
--------

- **spectral** -- Schuster's periodogram, the Wiener-Khinchin theorem, Gabor's short-time transform, Blackman and Tukey's windows, Goertzel's algorithm and Welch's averaged periodogram.
- **fourier** -- Gibbs's phenomenon and the Cooley-Tukey FFT.
- **wavelets** -- Haar's wavelet.
- **filters** -- Butterworth's filter, Hurewicz's z-transform, Wiener's optimal filter, the LMS adaptive filter, Kaiser's FIR design and second-order sections.
- **sampling** -- Bennett's quantization noise and Shannon's sampling theorem.

Designing, analysing and implementing digital filters, from fixed designs to adaptive ones.

    <div class="sphx-glr-thumbcontainer" tooltip="Stephen Butterworth, a British physicist designing filters for valve amplifiers, asked in 1930 for a response &quot;ideal&quot; in the passband. His answer, |H|^2 = 1/(1 + (\omega/\omega_c)^{2n}), has every derivative up to order 2n-1 zero at DC, so it is as flat there as an order- n filter can be. Each added order steepens the roll-off by 20 dB per decade, and the gain at the cutoff is always 1/\sqrt2 (-3 dB). Digital versions come from the bilinear transform, which maps the analogue frequency axis onto the unit circle through \omega = \tan(\pi f/f_s) (~engineeringkit.signal_processing.butterworth_lowpass, butterworth_highpass).">

In the Laboratory&#x27;s 1947 volume Theory of Servomechanisms, the topologist Witold Hurewicz described a sampled sequence by the generating function X(z) = \sum_n x_n z^{-n}, which Ragazzini and Zadeh named the z-transform in 1952. A difference equation becomes the rational function H(z) = B(z)/A(z). Its poles must lie inside the unit circle for stability, and on the circle z = e^{j2\pi f/f_s} it gives the frequency response (~engineeringkit.signal_processing.frequency_response, filter_signal).">

    <div class="sphx-glr-thumbcontainer" tooltip="Albert Michelson&#x27;s harmonic analyser (1898) summed 80 Fourier terms mechanically, and its curves for a square wave overshot at every jump. Michelson suspected the machine. Josiah Willard Gibbs (1899) showed in Nature that the mathematics itself overshoots: near a discontinuity the partial sums (~engineeringkit.signal_processing.fourier_partial_sum) rise about 9% of the jump above it, however many terms are kept. The overshoot only narrows toward the jump. Henry Wilbraham had found the same effect in 1848, unnoticed.">

.. raw:: html

    <div class="sphx-glr-thumbcontainer" tooltip="A direct DFT of N samples needs N^2 complex multiplications. James Cooley and John Tukey (1965) split it into the DFTs of the even- and odd-numbered samples, combined by &quot;butterflies&quot; E_k \pm W^k O_k, and repeated the split down to single samples (~engineeringkit.signal_processing.radix2_fft). The cost falls to (N/2)\log_2 N. Gauss had used the same idea in 1805 to interpolate asteroid orbits. In 1965 it made spectral analysis on a computer routine, and it is what dft (NumPy&#x27;s FFT) does.">

    <div class="sphx-glr-thumbcontainer" tooltip="Pulse-code modulation, worked out at Bell Laboratories during the Second World War, rounds every sample to one of 2^N levels (~engineeringkit.signal_processing.quantize). W. R. Bennett asked what that rounding does to the signal. For a signal that is busy compared with the step q (~engineeringkit.signal_processing.quantization_step), the error is spread evenly over [-q/2, q/2], so it behaves like added white noise of power q^2/12. For a full-scale sine this gives the rule every ADC data sheet quotes, \mathrm{SNR} = 6.02N + 1.76 dB: each extra bit buys 6 dB.">

Whittaker (1915), Nyquist (1928) and Kotelnikov (1933) had come close, but Shannon stated it plainly and gave the reconstruction, a sum of sinc pulses centred on the samples (~engineeringkit.signal_processing.sinc_reconstruct). Spectral analysis
-----------------

Estimating how a signal's power is spread over frequency, and how that spread changes in time.


.. raw:: html

  <div id='sg-tag-list' class='sphx-glr-tag-list'></div>


.. raw:: html

    <div class="sphx-glr-thumbnails">

.. thumbnail-parent-div-open

.. raw:: html

    <div class="sphx-glr-thumbcontainer" tooltip="Was there a 26-day period in the weather, linked to the Sun&#x27;s rotation? Arthur Schuster (1898) answered such questions with the periodogram: the squared magnitude of the record&#x27;s Fourier coefficients, |X_k|^2/N, plotted against period (~engineeringkit.signal_processing.dft, power_spectral_density). A true periodicity stands out as a peak that grows with the record length, while the noise level does not. Schuster also worked out how high a peak pure noise is likely to throw up. The example hides a weak sine in noise several times stronger and finds it.">

.. raw:: html

    <div class="sphx-glr-thumbcontainer" tooltip="Norbert Wiener (1930) and Aleksandr Khinchin (1934) showed that the power spectrum of a stationary random process is the Fourier transform of its autocorrelation function. A random signal has no Fourier transform of its own, but its correlation does, and that gives its spectrum a precise meaning. For a finite record the discrete version is exact: the DFT of the biased sample autocorrelation equals the periodogram (~engineeringkit.signal_processing.correlation, power_spectral_density).">

    <div class="sphx-glr-thumbcontainer" tooltip="A Fourier spectrum says which frequencies a signal contains but not when. Dennis Gabor (1946) described signals instead by &quot;atoms&quot;: Gaussian-windowed sinusoids, each occupying a cell of the time-frequency plane. Sliding a window along the signal and taking a DFT of each frame gives the short-time Fourier transform (~engineeringkit.signal_processing.stft). The cells cannot be made arbitrarily small. For RMS widths \sigma_t and \sigma_f of a window&#x27;s energy in time and frequency, \sigma_t\sigma_f \ge 1/(4\pi), and only the Gaussian reaches the bound.">

    <div class="sphx-glr-thumbcontainer" tooltip="Ralph Blackman and John Tukey&#x27;s The Measurement of Power Spectra (1958) turned spectral estimation into an engineering procedure for the telephone and seismic data of the Bell System. Cutting a finite record out of a signal multiplies it by a rectangular window. In frequency, every tone is then smeared by that window&#x27;s transform, whose sidelobes fall only slowly, so a strong tone can hide a weak one nearby. Their book named the tapered windows of Julius von Hann and Richard Hamming that trade a wider main lobe for far lower sidelobes (~engineeringkit.signal_processing.windowed_spectrum).">

When only a handful of frequencies matter, it is cheaper than a full FFT and needs no buffer of samples. Its classic use is the touch-tone (DTMF) telephone keypad, where each key sends two tones, one from a low group and one from a high group, and the receiver has to measure only eight frequencies.">

Peter Welch (1967) cut the record into overlapping segments, windowed each, and averaged their periodograms, computed with the new FFT (~engineeringkit.signal_processing.welch_psd). Averaging K nearly independent segments divides the variance by about K; the price is a coarser resolution, f_s/L for segments of L samples.">

Wavelets

Bases that are local in both time and scale.

    <div class="sphx-glr-thumbcontainer" tooltip="In his 1909 dissertation, published in 1910, Alfréd Haar built an orthonormal basis from a single step function: the difference between the two halves of an interval, shrunk and shifted to every scale and position (~engineeringkit.signal_processing.haar_transform). It was the first wavelet, though the word came 70 years later. Each Haar function lives on a short interval, so a jump in a signal touches only a few coefficients at each scale. A sine, by contrast, extends over all time and smears a jump over every coefficient. The transform keeps the signal&#x27;s energy and is inverted exactly (~engineeringkit.signal_processing.inverse_haar_transform).">
