Sampling

    <div class="sphx-glr-thumbcontainer" tooltip="Pulse-code modulation, worked out at Bell Laboratories during the Second World War, rounds every sample to one of 2^N levels (~engineeringkit.signal_processing.quantize). W. R. Bennett asked what that rounding does to the signal. For a signal that is busy compared with the step q (~engineeringkit.signal_processing.quantization_step), the error is spread evenly over [-q/2, q/2], so it behaves like added white noise of power q^2/12. For a full-scale sine this gives the rule every ADC data sheet quotes, \mathrm{SNR} = 6.02N + 1.76 dB: each extra bit buys 6 dB.">

    <div class="sphx-glr-thumbcontainer" tooltip="Claude Shannon&#x27;s 1949 paper Communication in the presence of noise opens with a theorem: a signal with no frequencies above B is completely determined by samples taken every 1/(2B) seconds. Whittaker (1915), Nyquist (1928) and Kotelnikov (1933) had come close, but Shannon stated it plainly and gave the reconstruction, a sum of sinc pulses centred on the samples (~engineeringkit.signal_processing.sinc_reconstruct). A frequency above the Nyquist frequency f_s/2 (~engineeringkit.signal_processing.nyquist_frequency) produces exactly the same samples as one below it, its alias (~engineeringkit.signal_processing.alias_frequency), and no processing after the sampler can tell them apart.">
