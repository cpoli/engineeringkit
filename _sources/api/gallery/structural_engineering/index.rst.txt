Examples
========

.. include:: /_generated/nav/structural_engineering.rst

This gallery walks through every public feature of
``engineeringkit.structural_engineering``, one breakthrough per script:
axial and torsional members, beams, column buckling, stress transformation,
energy methods, trusses, plastic collapse and frames.

:doc:`Units, assumptions and numerical validation </guides/structural_engineering>`
gives the physical validity limits. Each script is self-contained and can be
run directly with ``python examples/structural_engineering/<section>/<script>.py``.

Sections
--------

- **beams** -- Galileo's cantilever, Navier's bending theory, Clapeyron's three-moment equation and Winkler's influence lines.
- **members** -- Hooke's law, Coulomb's torsion and Poisson's ratio.
- **stability** -- Euler's column and Engesser's tangent-modulus load.
- **stress** -- Mohr's circle.
- **energy** -- Castigliano's theorem.
- **trusses** -- Whipple's method of joints, Maxwell's count and reciprocal theorem, and the direct stiffness method.
- **plasticity** -- Kazinczy's plastic hinges.
- **frames** -- Hardy Cross's moment distribution and plane frames by direct stiffness.

    <div class="sphx-glr-thumbcontainer" tooltip="In the Two New Sciences (1638) Galileo asked how strong a cantilever is, and answered that its breaking moment grows with the width b and the square of the depth h, so a plank on edge is stronger than one laid flat by the ratio h/b. He assumed the whole section pulls with the same stress and pivots about its bottom edge, which gives M = \sigma bh^2/2. Linear elasticity (Navier, 1826) puts the pivot at the centroid with a linear stress profile, M = \sigma bh^2/6 (~engineeringkit.structural_engineering.rectangular_section_modulus): Galileo&#x27;s scaling law was right, his constant three times too large.">

    <div class="sphx-glr-thumbcontainer" tooltip="Continuous beams and building frames of the 1920s led to large systems of simultaneous equations that no engineer wanted to solve by hand. In 1930 Hardy Cross proposed relaxation instead. Lock every joint against rotation and apply the fixed-end moments. Then release one joint at a time: its unbalanced moment is shared among the members meeting there in proportion to their stiffness 4EI/L, and half of each share is carried over to the far end of the member. Repeat until the joints balance (~engineeringkit.structural_engineering.moment_distribution). The method fitted on a sheet of paper and was used for forty years. The example balances a three-span beam built in at its left end, checks the result against the plane-frame stiffness solution and plots how fast the unbalance dies away.">

    <div class="sphx-glr-thumbcontainer" tooltip="In 1676 Robert Hooke published an anagram, ceiiinosssttuu, and in 1678 its solution, ut tensio, sic vis: &quot;as the extension, so the force&quot;. Springs, wires and wooden rods all stretched in proportion to the weight hung on them. Written for a bar of length L, area A and modulus E (Young&#x27;s, 1807), the extension is \delta = PL/(AE) (~engineeringkit.structural_engineering.axial_deformation) and the stored energy is the triangle under the load-extension line, U = P\delta/2 = P^2L/(2AE) (~engineeringkit.structural_engineering.strain_energy_axial). The example repeats Hooke&#x27;s experiment on a steel wire and checks both.">

    <div class="sphx-glr-thumbcontainer" tooltip="In 1914 Gábor Kazinczy loaded steel beams built into concrete walls in Budapest. They carried far more than the elastic theory allowed: when the most stressed section yielded through its whole depth it went on turning at a constant plastic moment M_p = \sigma_yZ_p (a plastic hinge), and the load moved to other sections until enough hinges had formed to make a mechanism. The collapse load follows from the work equation of the mechanism (~engineeringkit.structural_engineering.plastic_collapse_load).     <div class="sphx-glr-thumbcontainer" tooltip="In an appendix on elastic curves to his Methodus inveniendi (1744), Leonhard Euler found the load at which a straight, pin-ended column can stay in equilibrium bent as well as straight: P_{cr} = \pi^2EI/L^2. Other end conditions change the length of the half sine wave, which is what the effective-length factor K expresses (~engineeringkit.structural_engineering.euler_buckling_load). Dividing by the area gives the Euler stress \pi^2E/(KL/r)^2, a function of the slenderness alone (~engineeringkit.structural_engineering.slenderness_ratio, radius_of_gyration). The example recovers Euler&#x27;s load as the smallest eigenvalue of the finite-difference column equation EIy&#x27;&#x27; + Py = 0, then plots the Euler hyperbola against the yield stress.">

    <div class="sphx-glr-thumbcontainer" tooltip="Tests on real columns of intermediate length failed well below Euler&#x27;s load. Friedrich Engesser (1889) argued that once the stress passes the proportional limit, a column bends against the material&#x27;s tangent modulus E_t = d\sigma/d\varepsilon and not against E, so P_t = \pi^2E_tI/(KL)^2. Since E_t depends on the stress it creates, the buckling stress solves \sigma = \pi^2E_t(\sigma)/(KL/r)^2 (~engineeringkit.structural_engineering.tangent_modulus_load). Engesser later proposed a &quot;double-modulus&quot; load, and Shanley (1947) showed that the tangent-modulus load is the one at which a real column starts to bend. The example builds the column curve of an aluminium alloy with a Ramberg-Osgood stress-strain law and checks the computed stresses against the defining equation.">

Trusses
-------

Pin-jointed trusses by statics and by stiffness.


.. raw:: html

  <div id='sg-tag-list' class='sphx-glr-tag-list'></div>


.. raw:: html

    <div class="sphx-glr-thumbnails">

.. thumbnail-parent-div-open

.. raw:: html

    <div class="sphx-glr-thumbcontainer" tooltip="American railway bridges of the 1840s were built from rules of thumb, and some fell down. Squire Whipple, in A Work on Bridge Building (1847), showed that the force in every member of a pin-jointed truss follows from statics alone. Each joint is in equilibrium, so its two equations, \sum F_x = 0 and \sum F_y = 0, give the forces in two members. Start at a support and work across the truss, two unknowns at a time (~engineeringkit.structural_engineering.method_of_joints). The example solves a six-panel Pratt truss under a train of panel loads and checks the chord forces against the method of sections: at any vertical cut, the chord force is the bending moment divided by the truss depth.">

.. raw:: html

    <div class="sphx-glr-thumbcontainer" tooltip="James Clerk Maxwell&#x27;s short 1864 paper &quot;On the calculation of the equilibrium and stiffness of frames&quot; made two contributions. First, a counting rule: a plane frame of j joints needs 2j - 3 bars to be stiff, or, counting the r support reactions, m + r = 2j for a statically determinate truss (~engineeringkit.structural_engineering.truss_determinacy). The count is necessary but not sufficient, since bars can be placed where they are not needed. Second, the reciprocal theorem: the deflection at joint i caused by a unit load at joint j equals the deflection at j caused by a unit load at i (~engineeringkit.structural_engineering.maxwell_betti_reciprocity). Betti generalised it in 1872. The example counts four trusses, shows one that passes the count and is still a mechanism, and checks reciprocity on a statically indeterminate truss.">

.. raw:: html

    <div class="sphx-glr-thumbcontainer" tooltip="At Boeing in the early 1950s, M. J. Turner, R. W. Clough, H. C. Martin and L. J. Topp needed the stiffness of swept jet wings, which hand methods could not give. Their 1956 paper wrote down a stiffness matrix for each element, added the matrices into one global matrix K according to which joints each element connects, and solved K_{ff}u_f = F_f on a computer. This is the direct stiffness method, the seed of the finite element method (~engineeringkit.structural_engineering.PlanarTruss). It handles statically indeterminate trusses as easily as determinate ones, but it fails without warning near a mechanism. The solver therefore reports \operatorname{cond}(K_{ff}) and warns when it is large. The example checks a redundant bridge truss against Clapeyron&#x27;s energy theorem, then flattens a joint until the warning fires.">
