    <div class="sphx-glr-thumbcontainer" tooltip="In 1676 Robert Hooke published an anagram, ceiiinosssttuu, and in 1678 its solution, ut tensio, sic vis: &quot;as the extension, so the force&quot;. Springs, wires and wooden rods all stretched in proportion to the weight hung on them. Written for a bar of length L, area A and modulus E (Young&#x27;s, 1807), the extension is \delta = PL/(AE) (~engineeringkit.structural_engineering.axial_deformation) and the stored energy is the triangle under the load-extension line, U = P\delta/2 = P^2L/(2AE) (~engineeringkit.structural_engineering.strain_energy_axial). The example repeats Hooke&#x27;s experiment on a steel wire and checks both.">

    <div class="sphx-glr-thumbcontainer" tooltip="Charles-Augustin Coulomb (1784) hung a weight on a thin wire, twisted it and timed the oscillations. The restoring torque was proportional to the angle of twist, to the fourth power of the wire&#x27;s diameter and inversely to its length, which in modern form is \phi = TL/(GJ) with J = \pi d^4/32 (~engineeringkit.structural_engineering.torsion_angle). The same torsion balance later weighed the electric force and, in Cavendish&#x27;s hands, the Earth. The example simulates Coulomb&#x27;s pendulum and recovers the shear modulus from the period, then shows the shear stress across the wire (~engineeringkit.structural_engineering.torsion_shear_stress).">

    <div class="sphx-glr-thumbcontainer" tooltip="A bar stretched along its axis gets thinner. Siméon Denis Poisson&#x27;s 1829 molecular theory of elasticity predicted that the lateral strain is always one quarter of the axial strain. Measurements soon showed that the ratio \nu depends on the material: about 0.3 for steel, nearly 0.5 for rubber and nearly zero for cork, which is why cork seals a wine bottle without jamming in the neck. The lateral strain is -\nu\varepsilon (~engineeringkit.structural_engineering.poisson_lateral_strain), so the volume changes by (1 - 2\nu)\varepsilon to first order: an incompressible material has \nu = 1/2.">

    <div class="sphx-glr-thumbcontainer" tooltip="Coulomb&#x27;s \tau = Tr/J holds only for circular shafts. Rudolf Bredt (1896) showed that in any closed thin-walled tube the shear flow q = \tau t is the same all round the wall, q = T/(2A_m) with A_m the area enclosed by the wall&#x27;s centreline (~engineeringkit.structural_engineering.thin_walled_torsion_shear_stress), and that the twist is \phi = (TL/4A_m^2G)\oint ds/t (~engineeringkit.structural_engineering.thin_walled_torsion_angle). Aircraft fuselages, box girders and car chassis rails are designed with these two formulas. The example checks them against Coulomb for a circular tube, then cuts the tube along its length: the open section, with torsion constant st^3/3, is hundreds of times more flexible.">
