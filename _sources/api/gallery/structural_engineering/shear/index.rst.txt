    <div class="sphx-glr-thumbcontainer" tooltip="Building timber railway bridges in Russia, Dmitrii Ivanovich Jourawski saw large oak beams split along their length at mid-depth, where the bending stress is zero. Balancing the change of bending force on the part of the section above a cut, he found the shear stress on it, \tau = VQ/(It) (~engineeringkit.structural_engineering.transverse_shear_stress), and the force per unit length the keys or bolts of a built-up beam must carry, q = VQ/I (~engineeringkit.structural_engineering.shear_flow). The example draws the shear stress in a rectangle and an I-beam, checks that it adds up to the shear force, and spaces the bolts of a flitch beam, timber with a steel plate, using its transformed section (~engineeringkit.structural_engineering.composite_section).">

Robert Maillart (1921) explained both. In a section without two axes of symmetry the neutral axis is not perpendicular to the plane of the loads unless the loads act along a principal axis (~engineeringkit.structural_engineering.unsymmetrical_bending_stress, neutral_axis_angle); and the shear flows in the flanges of a channel form a couple, so the load must pass through a point outside the web, the shear centre (~engineeringkit.structural_engineering.channel_shear_center), to bend the beam without twisting it. The example computes both for a channel and an angle, built from rectangles by channel_section and angle_section.">
