Examples
========

.. include:: /_generated/nav/thermodynamics.rst

This gallery walks through every public feature of
``engineeringkit.thermodynamics``, one breakthrough per script: the first and
second laws, ideal and real gases and their mixtures, vapour pressure and
steam properties, heat-engine cycles and power plants, refrigerators and heat
pumps, exergy, moist air and combustion.

:doc:`Units, assumptions and numerical validation </guides/thermodynamics>`
gives the physical validity limits. Each script is self-contained and can be
run directly with ``python examples/thermodynamics/<section>/<script>.py``.

Sections
--------

- **laws** -- Joule's mechanical equivalent of heat and steady-flow
  devices, Clausius's entropy and Maxwell's relations.
- **properties** -- Dalton's partial pressures, Clapeyron's ideal gas, the
  Clausius-Clapeyron relation, the Joule-Thomson effect, van der Waals's
  equation, Antoine's equation, Pitzer's acentric factor, Peng and
  Robinson's equation and IAPWS-IF97 steam properties up to region 5.
- **cycles** -- Carnot's bound, Stirling's regenerator, the Rankine,
  Brayton, Otto, Diesel and dual cycles with reheat, feedwater heating,
  regeneration and intercooling, and the Curzon-Ahlborn efficiency at
  maximum power.
- **refrigeration** -- Perkins's vapour-compression refrigerator, on water
  and on R-134a, and Kelvin's heat pump.
- **exergy** -- Gouy and Stodola's lost work and Rant's exergy.
- **psychrometrics** -- Carrier's psychrometric chart and air-conditioning
  processes.
- **combustion** -- Hess's law, heating values and the adiabatic flame
  temperature.

    <div class="sphx-glr-thumbcontainer" tooltip="Sadi Carnot (1824) argued that a heat engine&#x27;s work comes from heat falling from a hot body to a cold one, as a water wheel&#x27;s comes from falling water, and that a reversible engine between two temperatures is the best possible. If some engine did better, it could drive the reversible engine backwards as a heat pump and, with no work left over, move heat from cold to hot. In modern form the bound is \eta_C = 1 - T_c/T_h (~engineeringkit.thermodynamics.carnot_efficiency), with absolute temperatures. The example runs Carnot&#x27;s argument with numbers and shows how the bound rises with the boiler temperature.">

Work potential and the work lost to irreversibility.

    <div class="sphx-glr-thumbcontainer" tooltip="Georges Gouy (1889) asked how much work a system can deliver when the only other body available is the environment at T_0. He showed that every irreversibility costs work equal to T_0 times the entropy it generates. Aurel Stodola reached the same result independently for steam turbines, in the editions of Die Dampfturbinen from 1898 to 1905. The Gouy-Stodola theorem, W_{lost} = T_0 S_{gen} (~engineeringkit.thermodynamics.exergy_destruction), lets an engineer rank the losses of a plant in a single currency. The example prices three irreversibilities: heat crossing a temperature difference, a throttling valve, and an imperfect turbine.">

    <div class="sphx-glr-thumbcontainer" tooltip="James Prescott Joule spent the 1840s showing that a given amount of work, however it is done, always produces the same amount of heat. In his best known experiment (published 1850) falling weights turned a paddle wheel in an insulated can of water, and he measured the temperature rise to a hundredth of a degree. His result, 772 foot-pounds per British thermal unit, made heat and work two forms of one conserved quantity, energy: Q = \Delta U + W (~engineeringkit.thermodynamics.first_law_heat). The example redoes the paddle-wheel balance, converts Joule&#x27;s number to SI, and checks the ideal-gas energy functions.">

    <div class="sphx-glr-thumbcontainer" tooltip="Boyle (1662) and Mariotte found that at fixed temperature a gas&#x27;s pressure times its volume stays constant. Gay-Lussac (1802) found that at fixed pressure every gas expands by the same fraction of its volume at 0 °C, about 1/267 per degree. In his 1834 memoir on Carnot&#x27;s theory, Émile Clapeyron combined the two as pv = R(267 + t). Regnault&#x27;s better measurements moved the 267 to 273, and with absolute temperature and the amount of gas the law reads pV = nRT (~engineeringkit.thermodynamics.ideal_gas_pressure, ideal_gas_temperature). The example rebuilds the absolute zero from a gas thermometer.">

From 1852 Joule and William Thomson forced gas steadily through a porous plug and measured the temperature on each side. The flow keeps its enthalpy, and real gases came out slightly cooler: air by about a quarter of a kelvin per atmosphere, carbon dioxide by about one. Hydrogen came out warmer. In 1997 the International Association for the Properties of Water and Steam adopted the Industrial Formulation IAPWS-IF97, led by Wolfgang Wagner.     <div class="sphx-glr-thumbcontainer" tooltip="In 1834 Jacob Perkins, an American engineer in London, patented a machine that evaporated ether at low pressure to absorb heat, compressed the vapour with a hand pump, and condensed it again under pressure. It was the first closed vapour-compression cycle, the cycle of nearly every refrigerator and air conditioner since. Its figure of merit is the coefficient of performance, the heat removed per unit of work, \mathrm{COP}_R = Q_c/W (~engineeringkit.thermodynamics.refrigeration_cop), and the second law bounds it by T_c/(T_h - T_c) (~engineeringkit.thermodynamics.carnot_cop_refrigerator). The example runs the ideal cycle with water as the refrigerant (R718, used today in some large chillers), because IAPWS-IF97 gives its properties.">

A refrigerator run backwards would warm a building with heat drawn from the cold outside air, and the heat delivered would be many times the work spent. He proposed an air machine for it: compress outside air, let it give its heat to the rooms, then expand it.
