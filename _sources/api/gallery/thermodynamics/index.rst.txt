Examples
========

.. include:: /_generated/nav/thermodynamics.rst

This gallery walks through every public feature of
``engineeringkit.thermodynamics``, one breakthrough per script: the first and
second laws, ideal and real gases, vapour pressure and steam properties,
heat-engine cycles, refrigerators and heat pumps, exergy and moist air.

:doc:`Units, assumptions and numerical validation </guides/thermodynamics>`
gives the physical validity limits. Each script is self-contained and can be
run directly with ``python examples/thermodynamics/<section>/<script>.py``.

Sections
--------

- **laws** -- Joule's mechanical equivalent of heat and Clausius's entropy.
- **properties** -- Clapeyron's ideal gas, the Clausius-Clapeyron relation,
  the Joule-Thomson effect, van der Waals's equation, Antoine's equation and
  IAPWS-IF97 steam properties.
- **cycles** -- Carnot's bound, the Rankine, Brayton, Otto and Diesel
  cycles, and the Curzon-Ahlborn efficiency at maximum power.
- **refrigeration** -- Perkins's vapour-compression refrigerator and
  Kelvin's heat pump.
- **exergy** -- Gouy and Stodola's lost work and Rant's exergy.
- **psychrometrics** -- Carrier's psychrometric chart.

Cycles
------

Heat engines: Carnot's bound and the idealized cycles of real engines.

.. raw:: html

    <div class="sphx-glr-thumbcontainer" tooltip="Sadi Carnot (1824) argued that a heat engine&#x27;s work comes from heat falling from a hot body to a cold one, as a water wheel&#x27;s comes from falling water, and that a reversible engine between two temperatures is the best possible. If some engine did better, it could drive the reversible engine backwards as a heat pump and, with no work left over, move heat from cold to hot. In modern form the bound is \eta_C = 1 - T_c/T_h (~engineeringkit.thermodynamics.carnot_efficiency), with absolute temperatures. The example runs Carnot&#x27;s argument with numbers and shows how the bound rises with the boiler temperature.">

Because the water is pumped as a liquid, the pump needs about 1% of the turbine work, which is the cycle&#x27;s great advantage over gas cycles. The efficiency (~engineeringkit.thermodynamics.rankine_ideal_efficiency) is computed here with IAPWS-IF97 steam properties (~engineeringkit.thermodynamics.steam_properties) for a plant at 10 MPa and 500 °C condensing at 10 kPa.">

The engine itself lost out to Otto&#x27;s, but its cycle (also called Joule&#x27;s) is the cycle of every gas turbine and jet engine: isentropic compression, heat addition at constant pressure, isentropic expansion and heat rejection at constant pressure. Its four strokes (intake, compression, power, exhaust) are idealized as the air-standard Otto cycle: isentropic compression through a ratio r, heat added at constant volume, isentropic expansion and heat rejected at constant volume. The efficiency 1 - r^{1-\gamma} (~engineeringkit.thermodynamics.otto_efficiency, OttoCycle) depends only on the compression ratio. Otto&#x27;s first engine had r \approx 2.5; knock limits petrol engines to about 10-12 today.">

Exergy
------

Work potential and the work lost to irreversibility.


.. raw:: html

  <div id='sg-tag-list' class='sphx-glr-tag-list'></div>


.. raw:: html

    <div class="sphx-glr-thumbnails">

.. thumbnail-parent-div-open

.. raw:: html

    <div class="sphx-glr-thumbcontainer" tooltip="Georges Gouy (1889) asked how much work a system can deliver when the only other body available is the environment at T_0. He showed that every irreversibility costs work equal to T_0 times the entropy it generates. Aurel Stodola reached the same result independently for steam turbines, in the editions of Die Dampfturbinen from 1898 to 1905. The Gouy-Stodola theorem, W_{lost} = T_0 S_{gen} (~engineeringkit.thermodynamics.exergy_destruction), lets an engineer rank the losses of a plant in a single currency. The example prices three irreversibilities: heat crossing a temperature difference, a throttling valve, and an imperfect turbine.">

    <div class="sphx-glr-thumbcontainer" tooltip="Gibbs&#x27;s available energy, Gouy&#x27;s énergie utilisable and Keenan&#x27;s availability all described the same quantity: the work a stream of energy could deliver if brought reversibly into equilibrium with the environment. In 1956 the Slovenian engineer Zoran Rant proposed one short word for it, exergy, built like energy from the Greek ergon, work. For heat Q available at T_s with surroundings at T_0 it is Q(1 - T_0/T_s) (~engineeringkit.thermodynamics.exergy_heat). An energy balance says energy is never lost. An exergy balance shows where the work potential goes.">

Laws

The first law (energy is conserved) and the second (entropy never decreases).

.. raw:: html

    <div class="sphx-glr-thumbcontainer" tooltip="James Prescott Joule spent the 1840s showing that a given amount of work, however it is done, always produces the same amount of heat. In his best known experiment (published 1850) falling weights turned a paddle wheel in an insulated can of water, and he measured the temperature rise to a hundredth of a degree. His result, 772 foot-pounds per British thermal unit, made heat and work two forms of one conserved quantity, energy: Q = \Delta U + W (~engineeringkit.thermodynamics.first_law_heat). The example redoes the paddle-wheel balance, converts Joule&#x27;s number to SI, and checks the ideal-gas energy functions.">

.. raw:: html

    <div class="sphx-glr-thumbcontainer" tooltip="Rudolf Clausius (1854) showed that around any cycle the heat received, each part divided by the temperature at which it crosses the boundary, sums to zero or less: \oint \delta Q/T \le 0 (~engineeringkit.thermodynamics.clausius_inequality), with equality only for a reversible cycle. Then \delta Q_{rev}/T is the change of a state function, which in 1865 he named entropy. The example builds a Carnot cycle from an ideal gas, checks that its Clausius sum is zero, and shows that drawing the same heat across a finite temperature difference makes the sum negative.">

Properties
----------

Equations of state, vapour pressure and the properties of water and steam.


.. raw:: html

  <div id='sg-tag-list' class='sphx-glr-tag-list'></div>


.. raw:: html

    <div class="sphx-glr-thumbnails">

.. thumbnail-parent-div-open

.. raw:: html

    <div class="sphx-glr-thumbcontainer" tooltip="Boyle (1662) and Mariotte found that at fixed temperature a gas&#x27;s pressure times its volume stays constant. Gay-Lussac (1802) found that at fixed pressure every gas expands by the same fraction of its volume at 0 °C, about 1/267 per degree. In his 1834 memoir on Carnot&#x27;s theory, Émile Clapeyron combined the two as pv = R(267 + t). Regnault&#x27;s better measurements moved the 267 to 273, and with absolute temperature and the amount of gas the law reads pV = nRT (~engineeringkit.thermodynamics.ideal_gas_pressure, ideal_gas_temperature). The example rebuilds the absolute zero from a gas thermometer.">

  .. image:: /api/gallery/thermodynamics/properties/images/thumb/sphx_glr_plot_01_clapeyron_ideal_gas_thumb.png

.. raw:: html

      <div class="sphx-glr-thumbnail-title">Clapeyron's ideal gas law (1834)</div>
    </div>


.. raw:: html

    <div class="sphx-glr-thumbcontainer" tooltip="Clapeyron (1834) applied Carnot&#x27;s reasoning to a small cycle of boiling and condensing and found that the slope of the vapour-pressure curve is fixed by the latent heat, \mathrm{d}p/\mathrm{d}T = h_{fg}/[T(v_g - v_f)]. Clausius (1850) rederived it from the two laws and, treating the vapour as an ideal gas and neglecting the liquid volume, integrated it: \ln p is linear in 1/T (~engineeringkit.thermodynamics.clausius_clapeyron_pressure). The example checks the exact slope against IAPWS-IF97 steam and uses the integrated form to find where water boils on a mountain.">

.. raw:: html

    <div class="sphx-glr-thumbcontainer" tooltip="Joule&#x27;s free expansion of 1845 showed no temperature change, which was too coarse a test of whether a gas has internal energy apart from its temperature. From 1852 Joule and William Thomson forced gas steadily through a porous plug and measured the temperature on each side. The flow keeps its enthalpy, and real gases came out slightly cooler: air by about a quarter of a kelvin per atmosphere, carbon dioxide by about one. Hydrogen came out warmer. For a van der Waals gas at low pressure the coefficient is \mu_{JT} = (2a/RT - b)/c_{p,m} (~engineeringkit.thermodynamics.joule_thomson_coefficient_vdw). Attraction (a) cools the gas and the size of the molecules (b) warms it. The sign changes at the inversion temperature 2a/(Rb), which Linde&#x27;s air liquefier (1895) relied on.">

    <div class="sphx-glr-thumbcontainer" tooltip="In 1869 Thomas Andrews showed that carbon dioxide above 31 °C cannot be liquefied by any pressure, and below it liquefies at a sharp pressure. In his 1873 thesis Johannes van der Waals explained this with two corrections to the ideal gas: the molecules fill a volume b per mole, and they attract each other, which lowers the pressure by a/v^2: p = RT/(v - b) - a/v^2 (~engineeringkit.thermodynamics.van_der_waals_pressure). One equation then describes both gas and liquid, and has a critical point (~engineeringkit.thermodynamics.van_der_waals_critical_point). He received the 1910 Nobel Prize in Physics for it.">

Measured vapour pressures bend away from that line because the latent heat falls as the temperature rises. In 1888 Charles Antoine, a French naval engineer, added a third constant to the temperature, \log_{10} p = A - B/(C + T) (~engineeringkit.thermodynamics.antoine_vapor_pressure). It fits most liquids to within a fraction of a percent over a range of 100 K, and tables of Antoine constants are still standard in chemical engineering. In 1997 the International Association for the Properties of Water and Steam adopted the Industrial Formulation IAPWS-IF97, led by Wolfgang Wagner. It splits the fluid into five regions, each with its own fast equation fitted to the scientific formulation IAPWS-95. In the compressed liquid (region 1) and the vapour (region 2) the equation is a dimensionless Gibbs free energy g(p, T)/(RT), and every property follows from its derivatives. Near the critical point (region 3) it is a Helmholtz free energy f(\rho, T)/(RT) of density, and region 4 is the saturation line (~engineeringkit.thermodynamics.steam_properties, saturation_pressure). Refrigeration
-------------

Refrigerators and heat pumps, and their Carnot limits.


.. raw:: html

    <div class="sphx-glr-thumbcontainer" tooltip="In 1834 Jacob Perkins, an American engineer in London, patented a machine that evaporated ether at low pressure to absorb heat, compressed the vapour with a hand pump, and condensed it again under pressure. It was the first closed vapour-compression cycle, the cycle of nearly every refrigerator and air conditioner since. Its figure of merit is the coefficient of performance, the heat removed per unit of work, \mathrm{COP}_R = Q_c/W (~engineeringkit.thermodynamics.refrigeration_cop), and the second law bounds it by T_c/(T_h - T_c) (~engineeringkit.thermodynamics.carnot_cop_refrigerator). The example runs the ideal cycle with water as the refrigerant (R718, used today in some large chillers), because IAPWS-IF97 gives its properties.">

A refrigerator run backwards would warm a building with heat drawn from the cold outside air, and the heat delivered would be many times the work spent. He proposed an air machine for it: compress outside air, let it give its heat to the rooms, then expand it. The figure of merit is \mathrm{COP}_{HP} = Q_h/W (~engineeringkit.thermodynamics.heat_pump_cop), at most T_h/(T_h - T_c) (~engineeringkit.thermodynamics.carnot_cop_heat_pump). Practical heat pumps had to wait for vapour compression and cheap electricity; they now heat millions of homes.">
