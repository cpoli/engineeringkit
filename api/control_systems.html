engineeringkit.control_systems#

Domain hub · History · Examples · API reference

Classical stability, compensator design, PID with anti-windup, nonlinear loops, state feedback, LQR and estimation.

engineeringkit.control_systems: feedback, stability, frequency response, state space and optimal control.

Black’s feedback amplifier and Minorsky’s PID controller with Ziegler-Nichols tuning; Maxwell’s characteristic roots, the Routh and Hurwitz tests and Lyapunov’s direct method; Nyquist’s encirclement criterion, Bode plots, stability margins, the Nichols chart and Bode’s sensitivity functions; lead, lag and lead-lag compensators, system type and error constants; Evans’s root locus; Hazen’s second-order servo specifications; Padé dead-time approximation and Smith’s predictor; zero-order-hold and Tustin discretisation and Jury’s test; PID anti-windup; describing functions, limit cycles and the phase plane; Kalman’s controllability and observability, pole placement and Luenberger observers; and the LQR, discrete LQR and Kalman-Bucy Riccati designs. Models are continuous-time and SISO unless stated; SciPy supplies the LTI objects, Riccati/Lyapunov solvers, pole placement and step responses.

simulate_saturated(plant, T, u_min, u_max, anti_windup=None, tracking_time=None, reference=1.0)[source]#

Step response with a saturating actuator, without or with anti-windup.

The controller computes \(v = K_p e + z - K_d\dot y\), the actuator delivers \(u = \min(\max(v, u_{min}), u_{max})\), and the integrator state \(z\) obeys

  • None: \(\dot z = K_i e\), which winds up while \(u \ne v\);

  • "clamping" (conditional integration): \(\dot z = 0\) while the actuator is saturated and \(K_i e\) would drive it further, else \(K_i e\);

  • "back_calculation": \(\dot z = K_i e + (u - v)/T_t\), bleeding the integrator off at the tracking time constant \(T_t\).

Parameters:

  • plant (scipy.signal.TransferFunction or tuple) – Strictly proper plant \(G(s)\); with kd != 0 its relative degree must be at least 2.

  • T (array_like) – At least two increasing sample times [s], fine enough for a fixed-step RK4 integration.

  • u_min (float) – Actuator limits [input units], u_min < u_max.

  • u_max (float) – Actuator limits [input units], u_min < u_max.

  • anti_windup ({None, "clamping", "back_calculation"}, optional) – Integrator protection.

  • tracking_time (float, optional) – \(T_t\) [s] for back-calculation; defaults to \(\sqrt{T_iT_d}\) with \(T_i = K_p/K_i\), \(T_d = K_d/K_p\), or \(T_i\) without derivative action.

  • reference (float, optional) – Height of the reference step [output units].

Returns:

Time [s], output and actuator output u.

Return type:

ResponseResult

Raises:

ValueError – For an improper or biproper plant, a derivative on a plant of relative degree one, bad limits or an unknown anti-windup mode.

Notes

Derivative action acts on the measurement, so the reference step gives no derivative kick; within the limits the response then differs from simulate() by that kick only. Without an integral gain the integrator is absent and anti_windup has no effect. Inputs are raw SI numbers with no dimensional metadata; see Control systems: matrix contracts and numerical stability.

References

K. J. Åström and T. Hägglund, PID Controllers: Theory, Design, and Tuning, 2nd ed. (ISA, 1995), sec. 3.5.

Examples

>>> from engineeringkit.control_systems import transfer_function
>>> G = transfer_function([1], [1, 0])
>>> r = PID(2, 1).simulate_saturated(G, np.linspace(0, 20, 2001), -0.5, 0.5, "back_calculation")
>>> round(r.summary()["final"], 3), r.summary()["input_max"]
(1.0, 0.5)

engineeringkit.control_systems.design_lag(loop, phase_margin_deg, extra_deg=5.0, zero_ratio=10.0, w=None)[source]#

Lag compensator that lowers a loop’s crossover to where it has the required phase margin.

  1. Find the frequency \(\omega_c\) where \(\angle L = -180^\circ + \gamma + \delta\), the allowance \(\delta\) paying for the lag’s residual phase there.

  2. Choose \(\beta = |L(j\omega_c)|\), so that the lag’s high-frequency gain \(1/\beta\) makes \(\omega_c\) the new crossover.

  3. Put the lag zero at \(\omega_c/\) zero_ratio.

Parameters:

  • loop (scipy.signal.lti) – Open-loop model \(L(s)\), with its gain already set for the required error constant.

  • phase_margin_deg (float) – Required phase margin \(\gamma\) [deg], strictly between 0 and 90.

  • extra_deg (float, optional) – Allowance \(\delta\) for the lag’s phase at \(\omega_c\) [deg], between 0 and 45.

  • zero_ratio (float, optional) – Ratio of \(\omega_c\) to the lag zero [-], greater than 1.

  • w (array_like, optional) – Positive angular frequencies [rad/s] for reading the response.

Returns:

The compensator, both loops and the margins actually achieved.

Return type:

CompensatorResult

Raises:

ValueError – If the phase never reaches the target, or the gain there is already below one (a lag cannot raise it).

Notes

A zero a decade below \(\omega_c\) costs about \(\arctan 0.1 \approx 5.7^\circ\) there, which is why \(\delta = 5^\circ\) is the usual allowance. The price is a slower response: the crossover, and so the bandwidth, goes down. Inputs are raw SI numbers with no dimensional metadata; see Control systems: matrix contracts and numerical stability.

Examples

>>> from engineeringkit.control_systems import transfer_function
>>> r = design_lag(transfer_function([5], [0.5, 1.5, 1, 0]), 40.0)
>>> round(r.phase_margin_deg, 1), round(r.gain_cross_frequency, 3)
(39.9, 0.564)
engineeringkit.control_systems.design_lead(loop, phase_margin_deg, extra_deg=5.0, w=None)[source]#

Lead compensator that raises a loop’s phase margin, by Ogata’s frequency-response procedure.

  1. Read the present phase margin \(\gamma_0\) of \(L\).

  2. The lead must supply \(\phi_m = \gamma - \gamma_0 + \delta\), where \(\delta\) (extra_deg) pays for the extra lag at the crossover, which moves up.

  3. Put the peak lead at the frequency where \(|L| = \sqrt\alpha\), so that the compensator’s gain \(1/\sqrt\alpha\) makes it the new crossover.

Parameters:

  • loop (scipy.signal.lti) – Open-loop model \(L(s)\), with its gain already set for the required error constant.

  • phase_margin_deg (float) – Required phase margin \(\gamma\) [deg], strictly between 0 and 90.

  • extra_deg (float, optional) – Safety allowance \(\delta\) [deg], between 0 and 45.

  • w (array_like, optional) – Positive angular frequencies [rad/s] for reading the response; defaults to 20000 points from \(10^{-4}\) to \(10^4\).

Returns:

The compensator, both loops and the margins actually achieved.

Return type:

CompensatorResult

Raises:

ValueError – If the loop has no gain crossover, already meets the margin, or needs 90° or more of lead from one stage.

Notes

A single lead stage gives less than 90° and, in practice, about 60°; beyond that use two stages or design_lead_lag(). The achieved margin differs from \(\gamma\) by however far \(\delta\) misjudges the extra lag. Inputs are raw SI numbers with no dimensional metadata; see Control systems: matrix contracts and numerical stability.

Examples

>>> from engineeringkit.control_systems import transfer_function
>>> r = design_lead(transfer_function([40], [1, 2, 0]), 50.0)
>>> round(r.phase_margin_deg, 1), round(r.gain_cross_frequency, 2)
(49.8, 8.85)

Lead-lag compensator giving a chosen crossover frequency and phase margin.

The lead puts its peak at the chosen crossover \(\omega_c\) and supplies \(\phi_m = \gamma + \delta - 180^\circ - \angle L(j\omega_c)\); the lag then sets \(\beta = |L(j\omega_c)|/\sqrt\alpha\) so that the total gain there is one.

Parameters:

  • loop (scipy.signal.lti) – Open-loop model \(L(s)\), with its gain already set for the required error constant.

  • phase_margin_deg (float) – Required phase margin \(\gamma\) [deg], strictly between 0 and 90.

  • crossover_frequency (float) – Required gain-crossover frequency \(\omega_c\) [rad/s], positive.

  • extra_deg (float, optional) – Allowance \(\delta\) for the lag’s phase at \(\omega_c\) [deg], between 0 and 45.

  • zero_ratio (float, optional) – Ratio of \(\omega_c\) to the lag zero [-], greater than 1.

  • w (array_like, optional) – Positive angular frequencies [rad/s] for reading the achieved margins.

Returns:

The compensator, both loops and the margins actually achieved.

Return type:

CompensatorResult

Raises:

ValueError – If the required lead is not between 0 and 90°, or the loop gain at \(\omega_c\) is too low for a lag to be needed (then use design_lead()).

The lead buys phase at the crossover, the lag gives back the excess gain the lead adds, so the error constants are unchanged and the crossover lands where it was asked. The phase of \(L\) is read in \([-360^\circ, 0^\circ)\). Inputs are raw SI numbers with no dimensional metadata; see Control systems: matrix contracts and numerical stability.

engineeringkit.control_systems.discretize_tustin(sys, dt, prewarp_frequency=None)[source]#

Tustin’s bilinear discretisation of a transfer function, optionally prewarped.

Every \(s\) in \(G(s)\) is replaced by \(c\,(z - 1)/(z + 1)\) with \(c = 2/T\), the trapezoidal rule for integration, or \(c = \omega_0/\tan(\omega_0 T/2)\) to make the discrete response match the continuous one exactly at \(\omega_0\).

Parameters:

  • sys (scipy.signal.lti) – Proper SISO continuous-time model.

  • dt (float) – Sampling period \(T\) [s], positive.

  • prewarp_frequency (float, optional) – Frequency \(\omega_0\) [rad/s] to match, positive and below the Nyquist frequency \(\pi/T\).

Returns:

Discrete-time model in descending powers of \(z\) with dt set, normalised to a monic denominator.

Return type:

scipy.signal.TransferFunction

Raises:

ValueError – If the model is improper or the prewarping frequency is not below \(\pi/T\).

Notes

A pole \(p\) maps to \(z = (c + p)/(c - p)\), so a stable model stays stable and the imaginary axis lands on the unit circle. The whole frequency axis is squeezed into \([0, \pi/T)\): the continuous frequency \(\omega\) appears at \(\omega_d = (2/T)\arctan(\omega/c)\), which is why prewarping is needed near \(\pi/T\). Inputs are raw SI numbers with no dimensional metadata; see Control systems: matrix contracts and numerical stability.

References

A. Tustin, “A method of analysing the behaviour of linear systems in terms of time series,” J. IEE Part IIA 94 (1947) 130-142.

Examples

>>> from engineeringkit.control_systems import transfer_function
>>> Gd = discretize_tustin(transfer_function([1], [1, 0]), 0.1)
>>> Gd.num.tolist(), Gd.den.tolist()
([0.05, 0.05], [1.0, -1.0])

Jury’s table reduces \(P(z) = a_0 z^n + a_1 z^{n-1} + \dots + a_n\) one degree at a time. Each row is \(a_0 P(z) - a_n z^n P(1/z)\), divided by \(z\), and the polynomial is stable if and only if \(|a_n| < |a_0|\) holds at every step down to degree zero.

Each row is normalised to unit leading coefficient, and a relative tolerance of \(10^{-12}\) decides the boundary cases. See Control systems: matrix contracts and numerical stability.

engineeringkit.control_systems.smith_predictor(controller, plant, delay, order=3)[source]#

Equivalent controller of a Smith predictor around a delayed plant.

The plant is \(G(s)e^{-s\theta}\). The predictor feeds back the output of a delay-free model \(G\) and corrects it by the difference between the measured output and the delayed model output. The result is the single controller

\[C_{\mathrm{SP}} = \frac{C}{1 + C G\,(1 - e^{-s\theta})},\]

which makes the closed loop \(CGe^{-s\theta}/(1 + CG)\): the delay-free design, delayed.

Parameters:

  • controller (scipy.signal.lti) – Primary controller \(C(s)\), designed for the delay-free plant.

  • plant (scipy.signal.lti) – Delay-free plant model \(G(s)\).

  • delay (float) – Dead time \(\theta\) [s], positive.

  • order (int, optional) – Padé order used for the delay inside the predictor.

Returns:

\(C_{\mathrm{SP}}(s)\) with \(e^{-s\theta}\) replaced by pade_delay() of the given order.

Return type:

scipy.signal.TransferFunction

Notes

The predictor relies on a perfect model: mismatch in \(G\) or \(\theta\) reappears in the characteristic equation. It cannot stabilise an unstable plant, whose mode stays in the open loop. In a computer the delay is a buffer of past commands, not a Padé factor. See Control systems: matrix contracts and numerical stability.

References

engineeringkit.control_systems.stability_margins(sys, w=None)[source]#

Worst gain and phase margins over all crossovers of a loop’s frequency response.

Parameters:

  • sys (scipy.signal.lti) – SISO open-loop model \(L(s)\).

  • w (array_like, optional) – Positive angular frequencies [rad/s]; defaults to 20000 points from \(10^{-4}\) to \(10^4\).

Returns:

gain_margin [-], the margin at the phase crossover nearest instability (inf without a phase crossover); phase_margin_deg [deg], the margin of smallest magnitude over the gain crossovers (nan without one); gain_cross_frequency and phase_cross_frequency [rad/s], the crossovers at which those worst margins are read (nan if absent); and the arrays phase_cross_frequencies [rad/s] with their gain_margins [-], and gain_cross_frequencies [rad/s] with their phase_margins_deg [deg], over every crossover in increasing frequency.

Return type:

dict

Notes

A phase crossover is any frequency where the phase passes \(-180^\circ + 360^\circ k\). Its gain margin \(1/|L(j\omega)|\) is below one when reducing the gain by that factor reaches instability, as in a conditionally stable loop; the worst gain margin is the one with the smallest \(|\log \mathrm{GM}|\). Phase margins are wrapped into \([-180^\circ, 180^\circ)\); a negative one means the plot crosses the unit circle on the far side of \(-1\), and a phase lead of that size reaches instability. With one crossover and a stable open loop that signals an unstable closed loop, but an open-loop unstable loop that needs counter-clockwise encirclements can cross on both sides, so the worst phase margin is the one of smallest magnitude. Crossings are interpolated linearly in log-frequency between grid points, so a grid that misses a crossover misses its margin. Time delays need a rational approximation such as pade_delay(). See Control systems: matrix contracts and numerical stability.

References

H. W. Bode, “Relations between attenuation and phase in feedback amplifier design,” Bell Syst. Tech. J. 19 (1940) 421-454.
