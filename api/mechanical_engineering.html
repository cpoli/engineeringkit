engineeringkit.mechanical_engineering#

Domain hub · History · Examples · API reference

Moments of inertia and the parallel-axis theorem, work, kinetic energy, power and flywheels; gear trains and the Euler-Eytelwein belt-friction law; Coulomb’s square-thread power screw, Petroff’s journal-bearing torque, the Sommerfeld number and long-bearing eccentricity, and the Lundberg-Palmgren \(L_{10}\) life; the slider-crank, Grashof’s condition, four-bar loop closure and Freudenstein synthesis, and Denavit-Hartenberg forward kinematics; the damped oscillator (scipy.integrate.solve_ivp), transmissibility, Frahm and Den Hartog vibration absorbers and Holzer’s torsional method (scipy.optimize.brentq); and the Jeffcott rotor.

engineeringkit.mechanical_engineering: dynamics, machines, mechanisms and vibration.

Moments of inertia, work, energy and power; gear trains and the Euler-Eytelwein belt-friction law; Coulomb’s power screw, Petroff’s and Sommerfeld’s journal bearings and the Lundberg-Palmgren rolling-bearing life; the slider-crank, Grashof’s condition, Freudenstein’s four-bar synthesis and Denavit-Hartenberg forward kinematics; the damped oscillator, the tuned vibration absorber, Holzer’s torsional method and the Jeffcott rotor. Models are hand-written closed forms; SciPy’s ODE integrator and root finder are used where they are the standard tool.

engineeringkit.mechanical_engineering.freudenstein_coefficients(input_angles, output_angles)[source]#

Freudenstein’s coefficients from prescribed input and output angles.

Freudenstein’s equation

\[K_1\cos\theta_4 - K_2\cos\theta_2 + K_3 = \cos(\theta_2 - \theta_4)\]

with \(K_1 = d/a\), \(K_2 = d/c\) and \(K_3 = (a^2 - b^2 + c^2 + d^2)/(2ac)\) is linear in the \(K\). Three precision points determine them exactly; more are fitted by least squares.

Parameters:

  • input_angles (array_like) – Matching crank angles \(\theta_2\) and rocker angles \(\theta_4\) [rad], at least three pairs.

  • output_angles (array_like) – Matching crank angles \(\theta_2\) and rocker angles \(\theta_4\) [rad], at least three pairs.

Returns:

[K1, K2, K3] [-].

Return type:

ndarray

Notes

Inputs are raw SI numbers with no dimensional metadata; see Mechanical engineering: power, vibration and mechanism geometry.

References

F. Freudenstein, “An analytical approach to the design of four-link mechanisms,” Trans. ASME 76 (1954) 483-492.

Examples

>>> t2 = np.radians([40.0, 70.0, 100.0])
>>> t4 = fourbar_output_angle(1.0, 3.0, 2.0, 3.0, t2)
>>> np.round(freudenstein_coefficients(t2, t4), 6).tolist()

engineeringkit.mechanical_engineering.jeffcott_response(m, k, e, omega, c=0.0)[source]#

Steady synchronous whirl of a Jeffcott (Föppl-de Laval) rotor.

A disk of mass \(m\), whose centre of mass sits \(e\) from the shaft centre, spins at \(\omega\) midway on a massless shaft of lateral stiffness \(k\). With \(r = \omega/\omega_c\) and \(\zeta = c/(2\sqrt{km})\),

\[\frac{|z|}{e} = \frac{r^2}{\sqrt{(1 - r^2)^2 + (2\zeta r)^2}}, \qquad \phi = \operatorname{atan2}(2\zeta r,\ 1 - r^2)\]
Parameters:

  • m (float) – Disk mass [kg], positive.

  • k (float) – Shaft lateral stiffness at the disk [N/m], positive.

  • e (float) – Mass eccentricity [m], positive.

  • omega (float or array_like) – Spin speeds [rad/s], nonnegative.

  • c (float, optional) – External viscous damping [N s/m], nonnegative.

Returns:

Whirl radius [m] and its lag behind the unbalance [rad] at each speed, and the critical speed [rad/s]. Without damping the radius is infinite at the critical speed.

Return type:

JeffcottResult

Notes

Gyroscopic effects, internal damping and bearing flexibility are neglected. Inputs are raw SI numbers with no dimensional metadata; see Mechanical engineering: power, vibration and mechanism geometry.

References

H. H. Jeffcott, “The lateral vibration of loaded shafts in the neighbourhood of a whirling speed – the effect of want of balance,” Phil. Mag. (6) 37 (1919) 304-314.

Examples

>>> result = jeffcott_response(10.0, 1e6, 1e-4, [0.0, 1e4], c=100.0)
>>> round(result.critical_speed, 6), round(float(result.amplitude[1] / 1e-4), 4)

engineeringkit.mechanical_engineering.long_bearing_eccentricity(S)[source]#

Eccentricity ratio of an infinitely long journal bearing (full Sommerfeld solution).

Solves

\[S = \frac{(2 + \varepsilon^2)\sqrt{1 - \varepsilon^2}}{12\pi^2\varepsilon}\]

for \(\varepsilon = e/c\), the journal’s offset as a fraction of the radial clearance.

Parameters:

S (float or array_like) – Sommerfeld number [-], positive (see sommerfeld_number()).

Returns:

Eccentricity ratio [-] in (0, 1); it tends to zero (Petroff’s centred journal) as \(S \to \infty\).

Return type:

float or ndarray

Notes

The full Sommerfeld solution keeps the negative film pressures that a real oil film cavitates away, so it puts the journal at 90 degrees to the load. The short-bearing and finite-bearing solutions are not implemented. Inputs are raw SI numbers with no dimensional metadata; see Mechanical engineering: power, vibration and mechanism geometry.

References

O. Reynolds, “On the theory of lubrication and its application to Mr. Beauchamp Tower’s experiments,” Phil. Trans. R. Soc. Lond. 177 (1886) 157-234; Sommerfeld (1904), as for sommerfeld_number().

Examples

>>> eps = long_bearing_eccentricity(0.1)

engineeringkit.mechanical_engineering.tuned_mass_damper(m, k, mass_ratio, frequency_ratio=None, damping_ratio=None)[source]#

Design a dynamic vibration absorber for a mass-spring system.

With frequency_ratio and damping_ratio omitted, Den Hartog’s optimum tuning is used:

\[f = \frac{1}{1 + \mu}, \qquad \zeta = \sqrt{\frac{3\mu}{8(1 + \mu)^3}}\]

which makes the two fixed points of the response curve equal in height, \(\sqrt{1 + 2/\mu}\) times the static deflection, and puts the peaks near them.

Parameters:

  • m (float) – Primary mass \(m_1\) [kg], positive.

  • k (float) – Primary stiffness \(k_1\) [N/m], positive.

  • mass_ratio (float) – \(\mu = m_2/m_1\) [-], positive.

  • frequency_ratio (float, optional) – Absorber frequency \(\sqrt{k_2/m_2}\) over \(\omega_1\) [-], positive. Frahm’s undamped absorber uses 1, tuned to the forcing.

  • damping_ratio (float, optional) – \(c_2/(2 m_2 \omega_1)\) [-], nonnegative.

Returns:

Absorber mass, stiffness and damping, with amplification(r) for the primary mass’s frequency response.

Return type:

TunedMassDamper

Notes

Inputs are raw SI numbers with no dimensional metadata; see Mechanical engineering: power, vibration and mechanism geometry.

References

H. Frahm, “Device for damping vibrations of bodies,” U.S. Patent 989,958 (filed 1909, issued 1911); J. Ormondroyd and J. P. Den Hartog, “The theory of the dynamic vibration absorber,” Trans. ASME 50 (1928) APM-50-7, 9-22.

Examples

>>> tmd = tuned_mass_damper(100.0, 1e5, 0.05)
>>> round(tmd.frequency_ratio, 6), round(tmd.damping_ratio, 6)
(0.952381, 0.127267)
