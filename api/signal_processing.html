engineeringkit.signal_processing#

Domain hub · History · Examples · API reference

Sampling, aliasing and sinc reconstruction; uniform quantization and SNR; the DFT, a hand-written radix-2 FFT and Fourier partial sums; convolution and correlation; the periodogram, windowed spectra, Welch’s averaged periodogram, the short-time Fourier transform and Goertzel’s algorithm; Butterworth filters as polynomials or second-order sections (scipy.signal), window-method FIR filters with Kaiser’s formulas; the FIR Wiener filter (scipy.linalg.solve_toeplitz) and the LMS adaptive filter; and the Haar wavelet transform.

engineeringkit.signal_processing: sampling, Fourier analysis, spectra, filters, adaptive filters and wavelets.

Sampling, aliasing and sinc reconstruction; uniform quantization and SNR; the DFT, a hand-written radix-2 FFT and Fourier partial sums; convolution and correlation; the periodogram, windowed spectra, Welch’s averaged periodogram, the short-time Fourier transform and Goertzel’s algorithm; Butterworth IIR filters as polynomials or second-order sections, window-method FIR filters with Kaiser’s design formulas; the FIR Wiener filter and the LMS adaptive filter; and the Haar wavelet transform. NumPy’s FFT and SciPy’s filter design and filtering are used where they are the standard tool; the algorithms named after their inventors are written out.

engineeringkit.signal_processing.butterworth_lowpass(order, cutoff, fs, output='ba')[source]#

Digital Butterworth low-pass filter by the bilinear transform.

Parameters:

  • order (int) – Filter order \(n\), at least 1.

  • cutoff (float) – -3 dB frequency \(f_c\) [Hz], between 0 and \(f_s/2\).

  • fs (float) – Sampling rate [Hz], positive.

  • output ({"ba", "sos"}, optional) – "ba" for numerator and denominator polynomials in \(z^{-1}\); "sos" for a cascade of second-order sections.

Returns:

(b, a) [-], or an (n_sections, 6) array of [b0, b1, b2, 1, a1, a2] rows [-].

Return type:

tuple of ndarray or ndarray

Notes

\(|H(f)|^2 = 1/(1 + (\tan(\pi f/f_s)/\tan(\pi f_c/f_s))^{2n})\): the first \(2n-1\) derivatives vanish at \(f = 0\), and the gain is \(1/\sqrt2\) at \(f_c\) for every order. Above about order 8, or for a cutoff far below \(f_s/2\), the "ba" polynomials lose precision and the filter can come out wrong or unstable; use "sos". See Signal processing: sampled energy, spectra and quantization.

References

S. Butterworth, “On the theory of filter amplifiers,” Wireless Engineer 7 (1930) 536-541; L. B. Jackson, “On the interaction of roundoff noise and dynamic range in digital filters,” Bell Syst. Tech. J. 49 (1970) 159-184.

Examples

>>> b, a = butterworth_lowpass(1, 250.0, 1000.0)
>>> np.round(b, 12).tolist()
[0.5, 0.5]

engineeringkit.signal_processing.goertzel(x, frequency, fs)[source]#

Goertzel’s algorithm: the DFT of x at one frequency from a second-order recursion.

Parameters:

  • x (array_like) – Real samples [signal units].

  • frequency (float or array_like) – Frequency or frequencies to evaluate [Hz]; need not fall on a bin.

  • fs (float) – Sampling rate [Hz], positive.

Returns:

\(X(f) = \sum_n x_n e^{-j2\pi fn/f_s}\) [signal units]; at \(f = kf_s/N\) this is DFT bin \(k\).

Return type:

complex or ndarray of complex

Notes

The recursion \(s_n = x_n + 2\cos\omega\,s_{n-1} - s_{n-2}\), with \(\omega = 2\pi f/f_s\), costs one real multiplication per sample. One extra step with zero input and \(X = e^{-j\omega N}(s_N - e^{-j\omega}s_{N-1})\) finish it. It beats an FFT when fewer than about \(\log_2 N\) bins are wanted, as in a touch-tone (DTMF) decoder. See Signal processing: sampled energy, spectra and quantization.

References

G. Goertzel, “An algorithm for the evaluation of finite trigonometric series,” Amer. Math. Monthly 65 (1958) 34-35.

Examples

>>> X = goertzel([1.0, 0.0, -1.0, 0.0], 1.0, 4.0)
>>> round(float(X.real), 12), round(float(X.imag), 12)
(2.0, 0.0)

Orthonormal multilevel Haar wavelet transform.

Parameters:

engineeringkit.signal_processing.kaiser_order(attenuation_db, transition_width, fs)[source]#

Kaiser’s formulas for the FIR length and window shape that meet a stopband attenuation.

Parameters:

  • attenuation_db (float) – Required stopband attenuation (and passband ripple) \(A = -20\log_{10}\delta\) [dB], positive.

  • transition_width (float) – Width of the transition band \(\Delta f\) [Hz], positive and below \(f_s/2\).

  • fs (float) – Sampling rate [Hz], positive.

Returns:

(numtaps, beta): number of coefficients (int) and Kaiser window parameter \(\beta\) [-], for window=("kaiser", beta).

Return type:

tuple

Notes

With \(\Delta\omega = 2\pi\Delta f/f_s\), \(N - 1 = \lceil (A - 7.95)/(2.285\,\Delta\omega) \rceil\) and \(\beta = 0.1102(A - 8.7)\) for \(A > 50\), \(0.5842(A-21)^{0.4} + 0.07886(A-21)\) for \(21 \le A \le 50\), and 0 below. The formulas are empirical and usually land within about a decibel of \(A\). See Signal processing: sampled energy, spectra and quantization.

References

J. F. Kaiser, “Digital filters,” ch. 7 in F. F. Kuo and J. F. Kaiser (eds.), System Analysis by Digital Computer, Wiley (1966); J. F. Kaiser, “Nonrecursive digital filter design using the I0-sinh window function,” Proc. IEEE Int. Symp. Circuits Syst. (1974) 20-23.

Examples

>>> numtaps, beta = kaiser_order(60.0, 50.0, 1000.0)
>>> numtaps, round(beta, 4)
(74, 5.6533)
engineeringkit.signal_processing.lms_filter(x, d, n_taps, mu, initial_weights=None)[source]#

Widrow-Hoff least-mean-squares adaptive FIR filter.

Parameters:

  • x (array_like) – Real input samples [signal units].

  • d (array_like) – Desired output, same length [signal units].

  • n_taps (int) – Filter length \(L\), between 1 and len(x).

  • mu (float) – Step size [1/signal units squared], positive.

  • initial_weights (array_like, optional) – Starting weights, length n_taps [-]; zeros by default.

Returns:

Output, error, final weights and the weight history.

Return type:

LMSResult

Notes

For each sample, with \(u_n = [x_n, \ldots, x_{n-L+1}]\): \(y_n = w^T u_n\), \(e_n = d_n - y_n\) and \(w \leftarrow w + \mu e_n u_n\). The mean weights converge to the Wiener solution when \(0 < \mu < 2/\lambda_{max}(R)\); in practice \(\mu \ll 2/(L\,E[x^2])\) keeps the excess error small. See Signal processing: sampled energy, spectra and quantization.

References

B. Widrow and M. E. Hoff, “Adaptive switching circuits,” IRE WESCON Convention Record, part 4 (1960) 96-104.

Examples

>>> rng = np.random.default_rng(0)
>>> x = rng.normal(size=2000)
>>> result = lms_filter(x, 0.5 * x, 1, 0.05)
>>> round(float(result.weights[0]), 6)
0.5

  • x (array_like) – Samples, real or complex [signal units]; the length must be a power of two.

  • return_multiplications (bool, optional) – Also return the number of complex twiddle multiplications.

Returns:

DFT coefficients, equal to dft() up to rounding, and, if requested, the multiplication count \((N/2)\log_2 N\).

Return type:

ndarray of complex or tuple

Notes

Each stage combines the DFTs \(E_k\) and \(O_k\) of the even and odd samples as \(X_k = E_k + W^k O_k\) and \(X_{k+N/2} = E_k - W^k O_k\), with \(W = e^{-j2\pi/N}\). The count includes the trivial twiddle \(W^0 = 1\). See Signal processing: sampled energy, spectra and quantization.

J. W. Cooley and J. W. Tukey, “An algorithm for the machine calculation of complex Fourier series,” Math. Comp. 19 (1965) 297-301.

engineeringkit.signal_processing.stft(x, fs, window_length, hop=None, window='hann')[source]#

Short-time Fourier transform \(X_m(f_k) = \sum_n x_{n+mH}\,w_n\,e^{-j2\pi kn/L}\).

Parameters:

  • x (array_like) – Real samples [signal units].

  • fs (float) – Sampling rate [Hz], positive.

  • window_length (int) – Frame length \(L\) [samples], between 2 and len(x).

  • hop (int, optional) – Frame step \(H\) [samples], at least 1. Defaults to \(L/4\) (rounded down, at least 1).

  • window (str or tuple, optional) – Analysis window, as in windowed_spectrum(); Gabor’s choice is ("gaussian", std).

Returns:

Frame centre times [s], one-sided bin frequencies [Hz] and the complex coefficients [signal units].

Return type:

STFTResult

Notes

A window of duration \(\Delta t\) resolves frequencies only to about \(1/\Delta t\). For RMS widths of \(|w|^2\) in time and of \(|W|^2\) in frequency, \(\sigma_t\sigma_f \ge 1/(4\pi)\), with equality only for a Gaussian. Frames are not padded, so the first and last centres lie \(L/2\) samples inside the record. See Signal processing: sampled energy, spectra and quantization.

References

D. Gabor, “Theory of communication,” J. IEE 93, part III (1946) 429-457.

Examples

>>> result = stft(np.ones(16), 16.0, 8, hop=4, window="boxcar")
>>> result.summary()["frames"], float(abs(result.values[0, 0]))
(3, 8.0)
engineeringkit.signal_processing.welch_psd(x, fs, segment_length=256, overlap=None, window='hann')[source]#

Welch’s averaged, modified periodogram, written out segment by segment.

Parameters:

  • x (array_like) – Real samples [signal units].

  • fs (float) – Sampling rate [Hz], positive.

  • segment_length (int, optional) – Samples per segment \(L\), between 2 and len(x).

  • overlap (int, optional) – Samples shared by consecutive segments, from 0 to \(L-1\). Defaults to \(L/2\) (rounded down).

  • window (str or tuple, optional) – Window applied to each segment, as in windowed_spectrum().

Returns:

(frequency, psd): one-sided bin frequencies [Hz] and power spectral density [signal units squared per Hz].

Return type:

tuple of ndarray

Notes

Each segment’s periodogram is \(|\mathrm{FFT}(w\,x)|^2/(f_s\sum w^2)\), doubled for every bin but DC and Nyquist, and the segments are averaged. Averaging \(K\) nearly independent segments divides the variance by about \(K\) and widens the resolution to about \(f_s/L\). No detrending is applied, so the result matches scipy.signal.welch(..., detrend=False). See Signal processing: sampled energy, spectra and quantization.

References

P. D. Welch, “The use of fast Fourier transform for the estimation of power spectra: a method based on time averaging over short, modified periodograms,” IEEE Trans. Audio Electroacoust. 15 (1967) 70-73.

Examples

>>> f, psd = welch_psd(np.ones(8), 8.0, segment_length=4, window="boxcar")
>>> psd.tolist()
[0.5, 0.0, 0.0]
engineeringkit.signal_processing.wiener_filter(x, d, n_taps)[source]#

FIR Wiener filter: the weights that minimize \(E[(d_n - \sum_k w_k x_{n-k})^2]\).

Parameters:

  • x (array_like) – Real input samples [signal units].

  • d (array_like) – Desired output, same length [signal units].

  • n_taps (int) – Filter length \(L\), between 1 and len(x).

Returns:

Weights \(w_0..w_{L-1}\) [-], to be applied as filter_signal(w, 1, x).

Return type:

ndarray

Notes

Solves \(Rw = p\) with the biased sample estimates \(r_k = \frac1N\sum_n x_n x_{n-k}\) and \(p_k = \frac1N\sum_n d_n x_{n-k}\), by Levinson recursion (scipy.linalg.solve_toeplitz). The minimum mean squared error is \(E[d^2] - p^T w\). Signals are assumed stationary. See Signal processing: sampled energy, spectra and quantization.

References

N. Wiener, Extrapolation, Interpolation, and Smoothing of Stationary Time Series, MIT Press and Wiley (1949); N. Levinson, “The Wiener RMS (root mean square) error criterion in filter design and prediction,” J. Math. Phys. 25 (1947) 261-278.

Examples

>>> x = np.array([1.0, -1.0, 2.0, 0.5, -1.5, 1.0])
>>> np.round(wiener_filter(x, 3 * x, 2), 12).tolist()
[3.0, 0.0]
