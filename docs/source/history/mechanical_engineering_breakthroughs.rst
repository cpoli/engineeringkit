Breakthroughs in Mechanical Engineering
=======================================


.. include:: /_generated/nav/mechanical_engineering.rst

.. epigraph::

   "Give me a place to stand, and I will move the Earth."
   -- Archimedes, on the lever, as reported by Pappus of Alexandria

Mechanical engineering began as the craft of building machines and became
a science when their parts could be calculated before they were made.
Natural philosophers of the seventeenth and eighteenth centuries supplied
the dynamics: inertia, friction and the geometry of gear teeth. Engineers
of the nineteenth century turned machines into kinematic chains and
measured what friction and lubrication cost. The twentieth century added
vibration, rotating shafts, rolling bearings and the analytical design of
linkages and robots. The entries trace the ideas behind
:mod:`engineeringkit.mechanical_engineering`. Each links to the implementing
function and to a gallery example devoted to it.

1673 -- Huygens's Centre of Oscillation and the Parallel-Axis Theorem
---------------------------------------------------------------------

Christiaan Huygens built the first accurate pendulum clocks, and in
*Horologium oscillatorium* he worked out how a rigid body swings about a
pivot. The body behaves like a simple pendulum whose length runs to its
*centre of oscillation*, :math:`L = I_O/(md)`, where :math:`I_O` is the
sum of :math:`r^2\,dm` about the pivot, later called the moment of inertia.
Huygens showed that the pivot and the centre of oscillation can be swapped
without changing the period, the principle of Kater's reversible pendulum.
Jakob Steiner (1840) stated the general rule :math:`I_O = I_G + md^2` that
moves a moment of inertia from the centre of mass to any parallel axis.

*Implementation:* :func:`engineeringkit.mechanical_engineering.parallel_axis`,
:func:`engineeringkit.mechanical_engineering.disk_inertia` and
:func:`engineeringkit.mechanical_engineering.solid_sphere_inertia`. The
example sums :math:`r^2\,dm` over a disk, times a simulated compound
pendulum and computes the clock-bob correction.

*References:* C. Huygens, *Horologium oscillatorium* (Muguet, Paris,
1673), part IV; J. Steiner, "Von dem Krümmungs-Schwerpuncte ebener Curven,"
*J. Reine Angew. Math.* 21 (1840) 33-63.

1765 -- Euler's Involute Gear Tooth
-----------------------------------

Millwrights cut gear teeth by eye, and the gears ran roughly because the
speed ratio varied as each pair of teeth rolled through contact. Leonhard
Euler worked out which tooth shapes keep the ratio constant: the common
normal at the contact point must always pass through the same pitch point
on the line of centres. He showed that the involute of a circle meets the
condition. Its normal is tangent to the base circle, so two involutes touch
along a fixed line tangent to both base circles. The ratio then depends only
on the base radii, and an involute pair still runs true if its centres are
moved apart. Involute teeth took over in the nineteenth century, once they
could be generated by machine.

*Implementation:* :func:`engineeringkit.mechanical_engineering.gear_ratio`,
:func:`engineeringkit.mechanical_engineering.gear_output_speed` and
:func:`engineeringkit.mechanical_engineering.gear_output_torque`. The example
checks that the involute's normals are tangent to the base circle, that the
ratio survives a change of centre distance, and the power balance of a
two-stage train.

*References:* L. Euler, "Supplementum de figura dentium rotarum," *Novi
Comment. Acad. Sci. Petropol.* 11 (1765, published 1767) 207-231.

1769 -- Euler and Eytelwein's Belt-Friction Law
-----------------------------------------------

Why can a sailor with a few turns of rope round a bollard hold a ship?
Euler found that the tension in a rope sliding over a fixed drum grows by
:math:`dT = \mu T\,d\theta` on each element of contact. Integrated, this
gives :math:`T_1/T_2 = e^{\mu\theta}`: every extra turn multiplies the
holding force by the same factor. Johann Albert Eytelwein's 1808 handbook
brought the result to engineers. It fixes the largest tension difference,
and so the power, that a belt drive can carry before it slips. A V-belt
wedged in its groove grips :math:`1/\sin(\beta/2)` times harder.

*Implementation:* :func:`engineeringkit.mechanical_engineering.belt_tension_ratio`
and :func:`engineeringkit.mechanical_engineering.belt_speed`. The example
builds the exponential from small arcs of contact and compares a flat belt
with a V-belt.

*References:* L. Euler, "Remarques sur l'effect du frottement dans
l'équilibre," *Mém. Acad. Sci. Berlin* 18 (1762, published 1769) 265-278;
J. A. Eytelwein, *Handbuch der Statik fester Körper* (Berlin, 1808).

1785 -- Coulomb's Laws of Friction and the Power Screw
------------------------------------------------------

Charles-Augustin de Coulomb won the Paris Academy's 1781 prize for a memoir
on the friction of simple machines, published in full in 1785. Working at
the Rochefort dockyard with heavy sledges and ropes, he found sliding
friction proportional to the normal load, nearly independent of contact
area and only weakly dependent on speed, :math:`F = \mu N`. Applied to a
screw thread, which is an inclined plane wrapped round a cylinder, the law
gives the torque to raise a load. It also shows that a screw holding its
load without a brake cannot be more than 50% efficient.

*Implementation:* :func:`engineeringkit.mechanical_engineering.screw_torque_raise`.
The example solves the force balance on the unwrapped thread and plots
efficiency against lead angle.

*References:* C. A. Coulomb, "Théorie des machines simples," *Mém. Math.
Phys. Acad. R. Sci.* 10 (1785) 161-332.

1829 -- Coriolis Defines Work and Kinetic Energy
------------------------------------------------

Engineers judged machines by the "effect" they produced, measured in
different ways by different authors. Gaspard-Gustave Coriolis, in *Du
calcul de l'effet des machines*, named force times distance *travail*, work,
and took :math:`\tfrac12 mv^2` rather than Leibniz's :math:`mv^2` as the
measure of motion, so that the work done on a body equals its gain in
kinetic energy exactly. The same accounting covers rotation,
:math:`\tfrac12 I\omega^2` and :math:`P = T\omega`. It sizes a flywheel by
the energy it must give up between two speeds.

*Implementation:* :func:`engineeringkit.mechanical_engineering.work_constant_force`,
:func:`engineeringkit.mechanical_engineering.kinetic_energy`,
:func:`engineeringkit.mechanical_engineering.rotational_energy`,
:func:`engineeringkit.mechanical_engineering.power_torque`,
:func:`engineeringkit.mechanical_engineering.flywheel_energy` and
:func:`engineeringkit.mechanical_engineering.angular_momentum`. The example
checks the work-energy theorem for a pulled cart and a punch-press
flywheel against integrated equations of motion.

*References:* G. Coriolis, *Du calcul de l'effet des machines*
(Carilian-Goeury, Paris, 1829).

.. minigallery:: ../../examples/mechanical_engineering/dynamics/plot_02_coriolis_work_energy.py

1875 -- Reuleaux's Kinematics of Machinery
------------------------------------------

Franz Reuleaux's *Theoretische Kinematik* reduced every machine to a
*kinematic chain* of rigid links joined by pairs, with one link held fixed.
Fixing a different link of the same chain gives a different mechanism, an
*inversion*. The slider-crank chain yields the piston engine, the
quick-return mechanism and the oscillating cylinder. Reuleaux's symbolic
notation and his collection of some 800 teaching models made kinematics a
subject of its own. For the engine, the exact piston motion is not
harmonic. The second harmonic, of relative size :math:`r/l`, is a shaking
force that no counterweight on the crank can remove.

*Implementation:* :func:`engineeringkit.mechanical_engineering.slider_crank_displacement`
and :func:`engineeringkit.mechanical_engineering.centripetal_force`. The
example differentiates the piston position and checks the accelerations at
top and bottom dead centre.

*References:* F. Reuleaux, *Theoretische Kinematik: Grundzüge einer Theorie
des Maschinenwesens* (Vieweg, Braunschweig, 1875); trans. A. B. W. Kennedy,
*The Kinematics of Machinery* (Macmillan, London, 1876).

.. minigallery:: ../../examples/mechanical_engineering/mechanisms/plot_01_reuleaux_slider_crank.py

1877 -- Rayleigh's *Theory of Sound*: Free Vibration
----------------------------------------------------

Lord Rayleigh's two volumes gathered the vibration of strings, bars,
plates, air columns and bells into one linear theory, and set out the
behaviour of the damped oscillator :math:`m\ddot x + c\dot x + kx = 0`. It
rings at :math:`\omega_n = \sqrt{k/m}`, slightly slower when damped. He also
gave the energy method that bears his name: for an assumed shape of motion,
equate the peak kinetic and potential energies. Because the true mode
minimises the resulting frequency, a reasonable guess gives a close upper
bound. The method shows, for example, that a spring's own mass adds a third
of itself to the moving mass.

*Implementation:* :func:`engineeringkit.mechanical_engineering.natural_frequency`,
:func:`engineeringkit.mechanical_engineering.natural_frequency_hz`,
:func:`engineeringkit.mechanical_engineering.damping_ratio`,
:func:`engineeringkit.mechanical_engineering.damped_frequency` and
:func:`engineeringkit.mechanical_engineering.simulate_sdof`. The example
times simulated free decays and checks Rayleigh's spring-mass correction
against the exact frequency.

*References:* J. W. Strutt, Baron Rayleigh, *The Theory of Sound*, vol. 1
(Macmillan, London, 1877).

1883 -- Grashof's Condition for Four-Bar Linkages
-------------------------------------------------

The four-bar linkage is the simplest closed chain and the basis of most
mechanisms. Franz Grashof's *Theoretische Maschinenlehre* gave the
condition for a link to turn a full circle. If the shortest and longest
links together are no longer than the other two, :math:`s + l \le p + q`,
the shortest link rotates fully relative to all the others. Grounding a
link next to it gives a crank-rocker, grounding it gives a double crank and
grounding the link opposite gives a double rocker. If the inequality fails,
no link turns fully whichever is grounded.

*Implementation:* :func:`engineeringkit.mechanical_engineering.fourbar_grashof`
and :func:`engineeringkit.mechanical_engineering.fourbar_output_angle`. The
example tries to assemble all four inversions of a Grashof and a
non-Grashof chain at every crank angle.

*References:* F. Grashof, *Theoretische Maschinenlehre*, vol. 2 (Voss,
Leipzig, 1883).

1883 -- Petroff's Law of Bearing Friction
-----------------------------------------

Oil-lubricated railway journals did not follow Coulomb's law: their
friction rose with speed and viscosity and fell, as a coefficient, with
load. Nikolai Petroff treated the oil as a viscous film sheared across the
small clearance :math:`c` between a centred journal and its sleeve. The
friction torque is then :math:`2\pi\mu\omega r^3 L/c`, and the friction
coefficient is proportional to the bearing characteristic :math:`\mu N/P`.
The same year Beauchamp Tower's experiments for the Institution of
Mechanical Engineers showed that the film also carries the load on a high
pressure, which led to Reynolds's theory.

*Implementation:* :func:`engineeringkit.mechanical_engineering.petroff_friction_torque`.
The example compares Petroff's thin-film torque with the exact flow between
concentric cylinders and plots the Petroff line.

*References:* N. P. Petroff, "Friction in machines and the effect of the
lubricant," *Inzhenernyi Zhurnal* (St Petersburg), nos. 1-4 (1883), in
Russian; B. Tower, "First report on friction experiments," *Proc. Inst.
Mech. Eng.* (1883) 632-659.

.. minigallery:: ../../examples/mechanical_engineering/machine_elements/plot_02_petroff_bearing.py

1886-1904 -- Reynolds and Sommerfeld: Hydrodynamic Lubrication
--------------------------------------------------------------

Osborne Reynolds explained Tower's measurements. A journal displaced from
the centre of its bearing drags oil into a converging wedge, and in a thin
viscous film the pressure obeys what is now called the Reynolds equation.
The film pressure supports the load with no metal-to-metal contact, which
is why a well-lubricated bearing barely wears. Arnold Sommerfeld integrated
the equation in closed form for a long bearing. He showed that load,
speed, viscosity and geometry enter only through one number,
:math:`S = (r/c)^2\mu N/P`, which fixes how far the journal runs off-centre.
The Sommerfeld number is still the first quantity computed in journal
bearing design.

*Implementation:* :func:`engineeringkit.mechanical_engineering.sommerfeld_number`
and :func:`engineeringkit.mechanical_engineering.long_bearing_eccentricity`.
The example integrates the Reynolds equation numerically, recovers
Sommerfeld's load and friction formulas, and shows the friction tending to
Petroff's as the load falls.

*References:* O. Reynolds, "On the theory of lubrication and its
application to Mr. Beauchamp Tower's experiments," *Phil. Trans. R. Soc.
Lond.* 177 (1886) 157-234; A. Sommerfeld, "Zur hydrodynamischen Theorie der
Schmiermittelreibung," *Z. Math. Phys.* 50 (1904) 97-155.

1909 -- Frahm's Vibration Absorber and Den Hartog's Tuning
----------------------------------------------------------

Hermann Frahm, a German shipbuilder, cured the rolling of ships and the
vibration of machines with an auxiliary spring-mass system tuned to the
disturbing frequency. At that frequency the absorber's spring pushes back
with exactly the applied force, so the main body stands still. Two new
resonances appear on either side, however. Ormondroyd and Den Hartog (1928)
added damping to the absorber. Every response curve then passes through two
fixed points, and the best tuning, :math:`f = 1/(1+\mu)`, makes them equal
in height at :math:`\sqrt{1 + 2/\mu}`. Tuned mass dampers now steady
skyscrapers, bridges and power lines.

*Implementation:* :func:`engineeringkit.mechanical_engineering.tuned_mass_damper`.
The example shows Frahm's anti-resonance and checks Den Hartog's fixed
points and optimum peak height.

*References:* H. Frahm, "Device for damping vibrations of bodies," U.S.
Patent 989,958 (filed 1909, issued 1911); J. Ormondroyd and J. P. Den
Hartog, "The theory of the dynamic vibration absorber," *Trans. ASME* 50
(1928) APM-50-7, 9-22.

1919 -- Jeffcott's Rotor and the Critical Speed
-----------------------------------------------

Rankine (1869) had concluded that a shaft could not run steadily above the
speed at which it whirls, yet Gustaf de Laval's steam turbines did so with
slender, flexible shafts. Henry Jeffcott resolved the paradox with an
unbalanced disk on a massless elastic shaft. Below the critical speed
:math:`\sqrt{k/m}` the heavy side faces out and the whirl grows with speed.
At the critical speed only damping limits the whirl. Above it the heavy
side swings round to face in, and the disk settles to spin about its own
centre of mass. Jeffcott's rotor is still the starting point of
rotordynamics.

*Implementation:* :func:`engineeringkit.mechanical_engineering.jeffcott_response`,
:func:`engineeringkit.mechanical_engineering.rotating_unbalance_force` and
:func:`engineeringkit.mechanical_engineering.transmissibility`. The example
integrates the whirl orbit below, at and above the critical speed and
computes the force reaching the bearings.

*References:* H. H. Jeffcott, "The lateral vibration of loaded shafts in
the neighbourhood of a whirling speed -- the effect of want of balance,"
*Phil. Mag.* (6) 37 (1919) 304-314.

1921 -- Holzer's Method for Torsional Vibration
-----------------------------------------------

Diesel engines and the long shafts of ships and generators suffered
crankshaft failures from torsional resonance. Heinrich Holzer's tabular
method finds the natural frequencies without forming a determinant. Guess
a frequency, give the first disk unit twist, and march along the shaft,
adding each disk's inertia torque and twisting each shaft by the
accumulated torque. The frequency is natural when no torque is left at the
free end. The method needs only arithmetic, and it carries over to beams
and, as the transfer-matrix method, to the critical speeds of rotors.

*Implementation:* :func:`engineeringkit.mechanical_engineering.holzer_residual`
and :func:`engineeringkit.mechanical_engineering.holzer_frequencies`. The
example finds the modes of an engine, flywheel and generator and checks
them against the matrix eigenvalue problem.

*References:* H. Holzer, *Die Berechnung der Drehschwingungen* (Springer,
Berlin, 1921).

.. minigallery:: ../../examples/mechanical_engineering/vibration/plot_03_holzer_torsional.py

1947 -- Lundberg and Palmgren's Rolling-Bearing Life
----------------------------------------------------

Ball and roller bearings fail by fatigue of the raceway, and identical
bearings under identical loads fail at widely different times. Gustaf
Lundberg and Arvid Palmgren of SKF modelled the lives as a Weibull
distribution arising from the stressed volume below the surface. They
defined the *rating life* :math:`L_{10}`, reached by 90% of bearings, and
showed it varies as :math:`(C/P)^p`, with :math:`p = 3` for ball and
:math:`10/3` for roller bearings. Their theory became the basis of the ISO
281 bearing-life standard.

*Implementation:* :func:`engineeringkit.mechanical_engineering.bearing_l10_life`.
The example draws a simulated batch of Weibull lives and checks the 90%
survival and the cube law.

*References:* G. Lundberg and A. Palmgren, "Dynamic capacity of rolling
bearings," *Acta Polytech. Mech. Eng. Ser.* 1, no. 3 (1947).

1954 -- Freudenstein's Equation for Four-Bar Synthesis
------------------------------------------------------

Linkage design was a graphical art until Ferdinand Freudenstein wrote the
four-bar's loop closure in a form linear in three ratios of link lengths,
:math:`K_1\cos\theta_4 - K_2\cos\theta_2 + K_3 = \cos(\theta_2 - \theta_4)`.
Prescribing three pairs of input and output angles gives three linear
equations, and a linkage that generates a desired function follows from a
3 by 3 solve. Spacing the precision points at Chebyshev nodes keeps the
error between them small. Freudenstein's paper started the analytical, and
soon computer-aided, kinematic synthesis of mechanisms.

*Implementation:* :func:`engineeringkit.mechanical_engineering.freudenstein_coefficients`,
:func:`engineeringkit.mechanical_engineering.freudenstein_link_lengths` and
:func:`engineeringkit.mechanical_engineering.fourbar_output_angle`. The
example synthesises a logarithmic function generator and measures its
structural error.

*References:* F. Freudenstein, "An analytical approach to the design of
four-link mechanisms," *Trans. ASME* 76 (1954) 483-492.

.. minigallery:: ../../examples/mechanical_engineering/mechanisms/plot_03_freudenstein_equation.py

1955 -- Denavit and Hartenberg's Link Parameters
------------------------------------------------

Jacques Denavit and Richard Hartenberg proposed a notation for spatial
mechanisms with lower pairs. Each link frame is placed so that the next is
reached by four numbers, :math:`\theta`, :math:`d`, :math:`a` and
:math:`\alpha`, and one 4 by 4 homogeneous matrix. The position and
orientation of the last link is the product of one matrix per joint. Their
convention became the standard description of industrial robot arms, from
the Stanford arm and PUMA onward, and of their forward kinematics.

*Implementation:* :func:`engineeringkit.mechanical_engineering.dh_transform`
and :func:`engineeringkit.mechanical_engineering.forward_kinematics`. The
example checks the matrix product against hand-derived trigonometry for a
planar and a spatial arm.

*References:* J. Denavit and R. S. Hartenberg, "A kinematic notation for
lower-pair mechanisms based on matrices," *J. Appl. Mech.* 22 (1955)
215-221.
