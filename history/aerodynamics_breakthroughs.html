Breakthroughs in Aerodynamics#

Domain hub · History · Examples · API reference

“It is possible to fly without motors, but not without knowledge and skill.” – attributed to Otto Lilienthal

Aerodynamics turned from speculation into engineering in about two centuries. Bernoulli and Newton supplied the conservation laws; Cayley, Lilienthal and the Wright brothers reduced flight to measurable force coefficients; Kutta, Joukowski and Prandtl explained where lift and drag come from; and the compressible-flow results of Saint-Venant, Rankine, Hugoniot, Mach and de Laval prepared the ground for transonic and supersonic flight. This chronology traces the ideas behind engineeringkit.aerodynamics. Each entry links to the function that implements the result and to a gallery example devoted to it.

1687-1816 – Newton, Laplace and the Speed of Sound#

Book II of Isaac Newton’s Principia (1687) gave the first theory of sound: a pressure pulse travels at \(\sqrt{p/\rho}\). Newton assumed the compressions were isothermal, and his estimate came out about 15% below the measured value, a gap he papered over with unconvincing corrections. Pierre-Simon Laplace (1816) resolved it: the compressions are too fast for heat to flow, so they are adiabatic, and the gas’s stiffness is larger by the ratio of specific heats \(\gamma\). In a perfect gas the speed of sound becomes \(a = \sqrt{\gamma R T}\), and the Mach number \(M = V/a\) becomes the natural measure of compressibility.

Implementation: engineeringkit.aerodynamics.speed_of_sound() and engineeringkit.aerodynamics.mach_number(). The example compares Newton’s and Laplace’s predictions with the measured value at 20 °C.

References: I. Newton, Philosophiae Naturalis Principia Mathematica (London, 1687), Book II, Props. 48-50; P.-S. Laplace, “Sur la vitesse du son dans l’air et dans l’eau,” Annales de Chimie et de Physique 3 (1816) 238-241.

1738 – Bernoulli’s Hydrodynamica and Dynamic Pressure#

Daniel Bernoulli’s Hydrodynamica (1738) argued from the conservation of “live force” (kinetic energy) that the pressure in a moving fluid falls where its speed rises. Leonhard Euler later put the result in its modern form, \(p + \tfrac12\rho V^2 = \text{const}\) along a streamline of steady, inviscid, incompressible flow. The kinetic term \(q = \tfrac12\rho V^2\), the dynamic pressure, is the scale of every aerodynamic force, and the pitot-static tube that measures airspeed reads it directly.

Implementation: engineeringkit.aerodynamics.dynamic_pressure(). The example turns a fixed pitot reading into true airspeed at several altitudes of the standard atmosphere.

References: D. Bernoulli, Hydrodynamica, sive de viribus et motibus fluidorum commentarii (Strasbourg, 1738); L. Euler, “Principes généraux du mouvement des fluides,” Mém. Acad. Sci. Berlin 11 (1757) 274-315.

1809 – Cayley Separates Lift from Thrust#

George Cayley’s three-part essay On Aerial Navigation (1809-1810) abandoned the flapping ornithopter for a fixed wing that supports the weight, plus a separate means of propulsion that overcomes the resistance. The idea seems obvious now but was revolutionary at the time: it splits the aerodynamic force into lift perpendicular to the flight path and drag along it, and reduces steady flight to two equilibria, lift equal to weight and thrust equal to drag. Cayley measured both forces on a whirling arm and flew man-carrying gliders built on the principle.

Implementation: engineeringkit.aerodynamics.lift() and engineeringkit.aerodynamics.drag(). The example balances a light aircraft at every speed and finds the minimum-drag speed, where parasite drag equals lift-induced drag.

References: G. Cayley, “On Aerial Navigation,” Nicholson’s Journal of Natural Philosophy, Chemistry and the Arts 24 (1809) 164-174; 25 (1810) 81-87, 161-169.

Cayley’s separation of lift and drag in level flight (1809)

1839 – Saint-Venant and Wantzel: Isentropic Outflow#

Adhémar Barré de Saint-Venant and Pierre Wantzel measured air escaping from a pressurised reservoir through an orifice. They derived the first correct compressible form of Bernoulli’s equation by combining the energy equation with the adiabatic law \(p\rho^{-\gamma} = \text{const}\). In modern notation, the stagnation temperature and pressure of a perfect gas are \(T_0/T = 1 + \tfrac{\gamma-1}{2}M^2\) and \(p_0/p = (T_0/T)^{\gamma/(\gamma-1)}\). They also observed that the mass flow stops increasing once the downstream pressure falls below a critical value, the first sign of choked flow.

Implementation: engineeringkit.aerodynamics.stagnation_temperature() and engineeringkit.aerodynamics.stagnation_pressure(), both built on engineeringkit.aerodynamics.isentropic_factor(). The example shows that the compressibility error of a Bernoulli pitot formula grows like \(M^2/4\).

References: A. J. C. B. de Saint-Venant and L. Wantzel, “Mémoire et expériences sur l’écoulement de l’air,” Journal de l’École Polytechnique 16 (1839) 85-122.

Saint-Venant and Wantzel: isentropic stagnation states (1839)

1870-1889 – Rankine and Hugoniot: the Shock Jump Conditions#

A shock wave is a discontinuity a few mean free paths thick, too thin for the inviscid equations to resolve. W. J. Macquorn Rankine (1870) and, independently, Pierre-Henri Hugoniot (1887-1889) sidestepped its structure by applying conservation of mass, momentum and energy across it. The resulting jump conditions fix the downstream state from the upstream Mach number alone. For a normal shock in a perfect gas they give \(p_2/p_1 = 1 + \tfrac{2\gamma}{\gamma+1}(M_1^2-1)\) and a subsonic downstream Mach number. Entropy rises across the shock, so only compression shocks are physical, and stagnation pressure is lost.

Implementation: engineeringkit.aerodynamics.normal_shock_mach2() and engineeringkit.aerodynamics.normal_shock_pressure_ratio(). The example rebuilds the downstream density, temperature and velocity and checks all three conservation laws to round-off.

References: W. J. M. Rankine, “On the thermodynamic theory of waves of finite longitudinal disturbance,” Phil. Trans. R. Soc. 160 (1870) 277-288; H. Hugoniot, “Mémoire sur la propagation du mouvement dans les corps,” Journal de l’École Polytechnique 57 (1887) 3-97; 58 (1889) 1-125.

1883 – Reynolds and Dynamic Similarity#

Osborne Reynolds injected dye into water flowing through glass pipes and watched the streak stay straight at low speed and suddenly break up and fill the pipe at higher speed. The switch depended on the single combination \(\rho V D/\mu\), whatever the pipe or the fluid. Two geometrically similar flows with the same Reynolds number are dynamically similar. That principle makes wind-tunnel testing of scale models meaningful, and it led NACA to build its pressurised variable-density tunnel in 1923.

Implementation: engineeringkit.aerodynamics.reynolds_number(). The example compares the Reynolds numbers of insects, birds and aircraft and works out the tunnel pressure needed to test a 1/10-scale model at full Reynolds number.

References: O. Reynolds, “An experimental investigation of the circumstances which determine whether the motion of water shall be direct or sinuous,” Phil. Trans. R. Soc. 174 (1883) 935-982.

1887 – Mach and Salcher Photograph a Supersonic Bullet#

Ernst Mach and Peter Salcher used spark-illuminated schlieren photography to capture a bullet in flight. Ahead of it was a curved bow shock, and trailing from it a conical wave whose half-angle satisfied \(\sin\mu = a/V\). A source moving faster than sound outruns its own wavefronts, so their envelope forms the Mach cone. Mach’s name was later given to the speed ratio \(M = V/a\) (by Jakob Ackeret, 1929).

Implementation: engineeringkit.aerodynamics.mach_angle(). The example draws the wavefronts left behind by a subsonic, sonic and supersonic source and checks that the envelope angle is \(\arcsin(1/M)\).

References: E. Mach and P. Salcher, “Photographische Fixirung der durch Projectile in der Luft eingeleiteten Vorgänge,” Sitzungsberichte der Akademie der Wissenschaften Wien 95 (1887) 764-780.

c. 1888 – De Laval’s Converging-Diverging Nozzle#

Gustaf de Laval needed very high steam velocities to drive his small impulse turbines. He found that a nozzle that first converges and then diverges delivers a supersonic jet, which a purely converging nozzle never can. Quasi-one-dimensional isentropic flow explains it. The area-velocity relation \(dA/A = (M^2-1)\,dV/V\) makes subsonic flow accelerate in a converging duct and supersonic flow accelerate in a diverging one, so the flow can pass through \(M = 1\) only at a throat. The area ratio \(A/A^*\) then fixes the Mach number at every station, on either a subsonic or a supersonic branch.

Implementation: engineeringkit.aerodynamics.area_mach_ratio(). The example inverts the relation on both branches with a bracketing root finder and traces the Mach number through a nozzle.

References: A. Stodola, Steam Turbines (New York: Van Nostrand, 1905), for de Laval’s nozzle; J. D. Anderson, Modern Compressible Flow, 3rd ed. (McGraw-Hill, 2003), ch. 5.

1889 – Lilienthal’s Coefficients and the Polar Diagram#

Otto Lilienthal measured the forces on cambered wings on a whirling arm and in the natural wind. He published the results in Der Vogelflug als Grundlage der Fliegekunst as tables of dimensionless coefficients and as a polar diagram of normal against tangential force. Dividing a force by \(qS\) removes its dependence on speed and size. Lilienthal then flew about two thousand glides on wings designed from these data before his fatal crash in 1896, and his tables were the starting point for the Wright brothers.

Implementation: engineeringkit.aerodynamics.lift_coefficient(), engineeringkit.aerodynamics.drag_coefficient() and engineeringkit.aerodynamics.glide_ratio(). The example reduces noisy synthetic measurements at two arm speeds and shows them collapsing onto one polar.

References: O. Lilienthal, Der Vogelflug als Grundlage der Fliegekunst (Berlin: Gaertner, 1889).

1901 – The Wright Brothers Correct Smeaton’s Coefficient#

The Wrights’ 1901 glider produced only about a third of the lift predicted from Lilienthal’s tables and the lift equation \(L = kV^2SC_L\). That equation carried Smeaton’s 1759 air-pressure coefficient \(k = 0.005\) lb/(ft² mph²). In a home-built wind tunnel with balances that measured lift and drag ratios directly, the brothers tested about two hundred model wings and concluded that \(k\) was nearer 0.0033. The modern value, \(\tfrac12\rho\) at sea level, is 0.00256. With correct data they designed the 1903 Flyer’s wing and propellers by calculation.

Implementation: engineeringkit.aerodynamics.stall_speed(). The example converts each historical \(k\) into an implied air density and shows that Smeaton’s value under-predicts the Flyer’s minimum flying speed by about 30%.

References: W. Wright, “Some Aeronautical Experiments,” Journal of the Western Society of Engineers 6 (1901) 489-510; M. W. McFarland (ed.), The Papers of Wilbur and Orville Wright (New York: McGraw-Hill, 1953).

The Wright brothers, Smeaton’s coefficient and the stall speed (1901)

1902-1906 – Kutta and Joukowski: Lift from Circulation#

Martin Kutta (1902) and Nikolai Joukowski (1906) independently showed that the lift per unit span on a two-dimensional body in a steady inviscid stream is \(L' = \rho V \Gamma\). Here \(\Gamma\) is the circulation, the line integral of velocity around any closed curve enclosing the body. A sharp trailing edge fixes \(\Gamma\) through the Kutta condition: the flow must leave the edge smoothly. Potential flow could at last explain lift, though it still predicted zero drag (d’Alembert’s paradox).

Implementation: engineeringkit.aerodynamics.kutta_joukowski_lift(). The example integrates the Bernoulli surface pressure around a cylinder with circulation and recovers \(\rho V\Gamma\) and zero drag to round-off.

References: M. W. Kutta, “Auftriebskräfte in strömenden Flüssigkeiten,” Illustrierte Aeronautische Mitteilungen 6 (1902) 133-135; N. Joukowski, “Sur les tourbillons adjoints,” Bulletin de l’Institut Aérodynamique de Koutchino 1 (1906).

1904-1908 – Prandtl’s Boundary Layer and the Blasius Solution#

In an eight-page paper at the 1904 Heidelberg mathematics congress, Ludwig Prandtl argued that viscosity matters only in a thin boundary layer next to a surface, outside of which the flow is effectively inviscid. Within the layer the Navier-Stokes equations simplify. Prandtl’s student Heinrich Blasius (1908) found the similarity solution for a flat plate: the ODE \(f''' + \tfrac12 f f'' = 0\), whose wall shear gives \(C_f = 1.328/\sqrt{Re_L}\) and thickness \(\delta_{99} \approx 4.91\,x/\sqrt{Re_x}\). Boundary-layer theory resolved d’Alembert’s paradox and founded modern fluid mechanics.

Implementation: engineeringkit.aerodynamics.boundary_layer_laminar() and engineeringkit.aerodynamics.skin_friction_laminar(). The example solves the Blasius equation by shooting and recovers both constants.

References: L. Prandtl, “Über Flüssigkeitsbewegung bei sehr kleiner Reibung,” Verhandlungen des III. Internationalen Mathematiker-Kongresses, Heidelberg 1904 (Leipzig: Teubner, 1905) 484-491; H. Blasius, “Grenzschichten in Flüssigkeiten mit kleiner Reibung,” Zeitschrift für Mathematik und Physik 56 (1908) 1-37.

1918 – Prandtl’s Lifting-Line Theory#

A three-dimensional wing cannot carry lift up to its tips without shedding vorticity. Prandtl replaced the wing by a lifting line, a bound vortex of spanwise-varying strength, that sheds a sheet of trailing vortices. These induce a downwash that lowers each section’s effective angle of attack. The resulting integro-differential equation for the circulation distribution gives the finite-wing lift slope \(a = a_0/(1 + a_0/(\pi e AR))\). The slope approaches the 2-D value \(2\pi\) only as the aspect ratio becomes infinite.

Implementation: engineeringkit.aerodynamics.finite_wing_lift_slope() and engineeringkit.aerodynamics.finite_wing_cl(). The example solves Glauert’s Fourier form of the lifting-line equation for rectangular wings and compares the computed slope with the closed form.

References: L. Prandtl, “Tragflügeltheorie,” Nachrichten von der Gesellschaft der Wissenschaften zu Göttingen, Math.-Phys. Klasse (1918) 451-477; (1919) 107-137.

1918-1921 – Munk and the Minimum Induced Drag#

The downwash of the trailing vortices tilts each section’s lift backwards. The streamwise component is the induced drag, the price paid for generating lift with a finite span, and it equals \(C_L^2/(\pi e AR)\). Max Munk proved that for a given span and lift it is smallest when the downwash is uniform, which happens for an elliptic spanwise load (\(e = 1\)). Any other load shape adds harmonics and drag. The result explains the elliptic wings of the Supermarine Spitfire and the long, slender wings of sailplanes.

Implementation: engineeringkit.aerodynamics.induced_drag_coefficient(). The example computes \(e\) from the Fourier content of several spanwise loads and confirms that the ellipse minimises induced drag.

References: M. M. Munk, “The minimum induced drag of aerofoils,” NACA Report 121 (1921).

1919-1952 – The Standard Atmosphere#

Aircraft performance depends on air density, which varies with weather and altitude, so test results could not be compared without a common reference. Albert Toussaint proposed one in France in 1919. NACA issued the first U.S. standard atmosphere in 1925, and the International Civil Aviation Organization adopted the International Standard Atmosphere in 1952. It assumes sea-level conditions of 288.15 K and 101325 Pa, a 6.5 K/km temperature lapse up to the tropopause at 11 km, and an isothermal layer above. Hydrostatic balance and the ideal-gas law then fix pressure and density in closed form.

Implementation: engineeringkit.aerodynamics.isa_atmosphere() returns an AtmosphereState. The example checks hydrostatic balance numerically and shows the stall speed rising with altitude.

References: W. S. Diehl, “Standard atmosphere – tables and data,” NACA Report 218 (1925); ICAO, Manual of the ICAO Standard Atmosphere, Doc 7488 (1952); ISO 2533:1975.

1922-1926 – Munk and Glauert: Thin-Airfoil Theory#

Munk (1922) modelled a thin section as a sheet of vortices along its camber line, and Hermann Glauert (1926) gave the theory its classical form with the change of variable \(x = \tfrac12(1-\cos\theta)\) and a Fourier series for the vortex strength. Two results stand out. Every thin section has the same lift slope, \(2\pi\) per radian. Camber only shifts the lift line, by a zero-lift angle given by an integral of the camber-line slope.

Implementation: engineeringkit.aerodynamics.thin_airfoil_cl() and engineeringkit.aerodynamics.naca4_zero_lift_angle(), which evaluates Glauert’s integral by quadrature. The example reproduces the textbook value \(\alpha_{L=0} = -2.08°\) for the NACA 2412.

References: M. M. Munk, “General theory of thin wing sections,” NACA Report 142 (1922); H. Glauert, The Elements of Aerofoil and Airscrew Theory (Cambridge University Press, 1926).

Thin-airfoil theory: lift slope 2π and the camber shift (1922-1926)

c. 1920 – The Breguet Range Equation#

The range equation attributed to the French aviation pioneer Louis Breguet combines three facts about cruise. Thrust equals drag, drag equals weight divided by \(L/D\), and fuel burns at a rate set by the engine’s specific fuel consumption. Integrating the weight loss gives \(R = \frac{V}{g c_T}\frac{L}{D}\ln\frac{W_i}{W_f}\). Range is thus a product of three efficiencies: the engine’s, the airframe’s aerodynamics, and the structure’s (through the fuel fraction). The equation still frames every long-range aircraft design.

Implementation: engineeringkit.aerodynamics.breguet_range(), with engineeringkit.aerodynamics.best_glide_cl() giving the lift coefficient that maximises \(L/D\). The example checks the formula against a time-stepped cruise.

References: J. D. Anderson, Aircraft Performance and Design (McGraw-Hill, 1999), sec. 5.13.

1927 – Prandtl’s Turbulent Flat-Plate Friction#

Most aircraft surfaces carry turbulent boundary layers. Prandtl combined Blasius’s empirical pipe-friction law with a one-seventh-power velocity profile to estimate the friction on a turbulent flat plate, \(C_f = 0.074\,Re_L^{-1/5}\). It decays far more slowly with Reynolds number than the laminar law, so a turbulent layer can carry several times the friction drag. That is the reason behind later efforts to delay transition with laminar-flow airfoils.

Implementation: engineeringkit.aerodynamics.skin_friction_turbulent(). The example compares the laminar and turbulent laws and the mixed friction of a plate that transitions part-way along.

References: L. Prandtl, “Über den Reibungswiderstand strömender Luft,” Ergebnisse der Aerodynamischen Versuchsanstalt zu Göttingen III (1927) 1-5; H. Schlichting and K. Gersten, Boundary-Layer Theory, 9th ed. (Springer, 2017), ch. 18.

1928 – The Prandtl-Glauert Rule#

As propeller tips and then aircraft approached the speed of sound, pressure distributions measured at high subsonic speed no longer matched incompressible theory. Glauert showed that the linearised compressible potential equation reduces to Laplace’s equation in coordinates stretched by \(\sqrt{1-M_\infty^2}\). Every pressure coefficient and the lift slope are therefore amplified by \(1/\sqrt{1-M_\infty^2}\). The rule works well up to roughly Mach 0.7. Its divergence at \(M = 1\) fed the popular idea of a “sound barrier”.

Implementation: engineeringkit.aerodynamics.prandtl_glauert_cp(). The example compares it with the Kármán-Tsien rule (1939), which accounts for part of the nonlinearity.

References: H. Glauert, “The effect of compressibility on the lift of an aerofoil,” Proc. R. Soc. Lond. A 118 (1928) 113-119; H. S. Tsien, “Two-dimensional subsonic flow of compressible fluids,” J. Aeronaut. Sci. 6 (1939) 399-407.

1933 – The NACA Four-Digit Sections#

Before the 1930s, airfoils were drawn by hand and tested one at a time. Eastman Jacobs, Kenneth Ward and Robert Pinkerton defined a systematic family instead. A single thickness polynomial is laid perpendicular to a two-parabola camber line, so four digits specify the whole shape. They tested 78 related sections in the variable-density tunnel, making it possible to choose a section by its parameters. The NACA 0012 and 2412 are still used.

Implementation: engineeringkit.aerodynamics.naca4_airfoil() returns an Airfoil, drawn by engineeringkit.aerodynamics.visualizers.plot_airfoil(). The example recovers each designation from the generated geometry.

References: E. N. Jacobs, K. E. Ward and R. M. Pinkerton, “The characteristics of 78 related airfoil sections from tests in the variable-density wind tunnel,” NACA Report 460 (1933).

1933 – Oswald’s Efficiency Factor and the Airplane Drag Polar#

W. Bailey Oswald’s NACA Report 408 extended the induced-drag formula from an isolated wing to a complete aeroplane. He absorbed the non-elliptic loading, the fuselage and the lift-dependent parasite drag into one empirical efficiency factor, \(e\), giving the parabolic polar \(C_D = C_{D0} + C_L^2/(\pi e AR)\). The polar puts aircraft performance in closed form. Maximum lift-to-drag occurs where induced drag equals parasite drag, at \(C_L = \sqrt{\pi e AR C_{D0}}\).

Implementation: engineeringkit.aerodynamics.drag_polar(), engineeringkit.aerodynamics.oswald_efficiency_estimate() and engineeringkit.aerodynamics.best_glide_cl(), plotted by engineeringkit.aerodynamics.visualizers.plot_drag_polar(). The example draws polars for three aspect ratios with their best-glide tangents.

References: W. B. Oswald, “General formulas and charts for the calculation of airplane performance,” NACA Report 408 (1933).
